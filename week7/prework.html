MSF Financial Modeling

Week 7 — Prework

Register: TEACH. The bridge from two working models to a defence you can give in four minutes.

You arrive with: two companies valued to the same standard, one exhibit, a flip point, two triggers, and an Edition A page written before any of it.

This week you build nothing. Both sessions are defences, in the room, in front of the class. What you bring is finished; what you do here is stand behind it and answer for it.

Honest time budget: about three hours, and none of it is modelling.

Part What Time
A Finish company B to company A's depth 90 min
B Cut the defence to four minutes, out loud, timed 45 min
C Write your discussant's question for the other cohort 20 min
D Check what your numbers say against each other 25 min

Part A — Both companies, same depth

The capstone is comparative, and a comparison is only as good as its weaker half. Company A got Exercise 4's attention. Company B needs the same: the last filed year reproduced, the full driver set labelled, the scenarios, the sensitivity, the honest range against a dated price.

The test: could a reader tell which company you did first? If yes, B is not finished.

Part B — Four minutes, out loud, timed

Four minutes is about 550 spoken words. It is far shorter than it sounds, and the most common failure in this room will be a student still describing their company at the three-minute mark.

Cut to this, and cut everything else:
The pair and the decision which gets the capital, in one sentence 20 sec
One exhibit both ranges against both dated prices, and the gap for each as a percentage 60 sec
The flip point the assumption that reverses the ranking, the value at which it reverses, and whether that value is inside the company's own history 60 sec
Two triggers observable, threshold, review date, one per company 30 sec
One check that caught something what you believed, what the filing said, what you changed 30 sec
Buffer 40 sec

Banned, and it will be stopped: company background, a tour of your methodology, "I pulled the data from EDGAR", a slide of your code, an apology for what you did not finish.

One slide. The exhibit. Nothing else needs projecting, and a deck costs you the time you need.

Time it out loud, twice. Reading it in your head runs about 30% fast.

Part C — Your discussant question

You attend both sessions. On the day you are not presenting you are a graded discussant for one assigned classmate in the other cohort.

Before your discussant day, submit one written question for the person you are assigned. A good question is answerable only by someone who ran their own model:

  • Weak: "How did you choose your discount rate?"
  • Strong: "Your flip point is a gross margin of 15.9%. Your low case already runs a 16.2% margin. Why does the low case not already flip the ranking?"

The strong one names their number and finds the tension between two of them. Read their exhibit and find yours.

Part D — Do your own numbers agree with each other?

An hour spent here will save your defence. The room will find these, and so will the grader.

  1. Does the range in your memo match the range your notebook prints today?
  2. Is every market price dated, and is the date within a week of your submission?
  3. Does your stated terminal share match what the model computes?
  4. Does your flip point actually flip it? Rerun once more and confirm.
  5. Does Edition A's prediction match what you now believe? If it does not, that is a finding, not an embarrassment — the memo ends on exactly that comparison.

Bring to your presenting day: one slide, four minutes rehearsed, your notebook open and navigable. Bring to your discussant day: your submitted question, and something to write with.
