You arrive with: two companies valued the same way, one exhibit, a flip point, two triggers, and a page you wrote before you knew any of the answers. Everything the capstone needs exists; this week decides how much of it you can stand behind.

This week you do not build. You stand behind what you built, in four minutes, and answer two questions about your own numbers.

Why this matters

Analysts are not hired for their models. Models are checked by someone else and replaced within a year. Analysts are hired because someone in a room watched them defend a number under pressure and believed them.

The defence is the only part of this course that cannot be produced by an agent, delegated, or polished overnight. It is also the closest thing here to the job: a committee has capital for one position, you have four minutes, and the person across the table wants to know what would have to be true for you to be wrong.

Two things are being tested, and only two. Can you state a decision and its condition in one sentence. And can you find any number you have written.

Part 1 — Why four minutes is the right amount of pain

A real investment-committee slot is five to ten minutes for a position that took weeks. The compression is not an artefact of a 90-minute class with fourteen presenters; it is the actual job, and it is why the format feels brutal.

Compression forces the thing that grading cannot: you must decide what the point is. A student with eight minutes describes. A student with four minutes concludes.

The structure in lab.md is fixed for everyone, which means the room can compare fourteen defences on the same axes, and you do not spend your scarce seconds on scaffolding. What you choose to put inside each slot is where the judgment shows.

What the time actually costs

What Spoken length
Four minutes about 550 words
One sentence of company background 25 words, 5% of your slot
"I pulled the financial statements from EDGAR using the company-facts API" 13 words for information worth none
The flip point, stated properly with its value and its history about 60 words, and it is the graded core

Every sentence that is not load-bearing is taking seconds from the flip point. That is the whole editing rule.

Part 2 — Leading with the decision

Most people build up to a conclusion, because that is how the work happened. A committee reads and listens in the opposite order: conclusion, then condition, then evidence.

Weak, and common:

"So I looked at two companies in the auto retail space. I started by pulling their filings, and I built a five-year pro-forma for each. The models gave me ranges of about $240 to $330 for the first one and $95 to $140 for the second. Based on that I think the first one looks more attractive..."

Three quarters of the slot is gone and nothing has been decided.

Strong:

"We recommend A, and we hold that as long as its gross margin stays above roughly 16%. A trades at a 31% discount to our midpoint; B trades at 9%. Here is the exhibit."

Seventeen seconds, and the room now knows the decision, the condition, the magnitude, and where to look. Everything after it is evidence for a claim already on the table.

The rule this gives you. Say the decision in the first sentence, and the condition in the second. If your first sentence contains the word "so", you are still narrating.

Part 3 — What a committee actually asks

Not "what is your target price." The questions are always some version of where is this fragile, and they come in four shapes. You can prepare all four in an hour.

Shape What it sounds like What it is testing
Locate it "Show me the line that produces that." whether the model is yours
What would break it "What has to be true for the ranking to reverse?" whether you know your flip point
Why should I believe that input "Where did the terminal growth come from?" whether the inputs are sourced or invented
Why these two "Is this comparison fair?" whether the two models were built the same way

The two questions you get on the day are the first two shapes, aimed at your own cells. Your discussant's question is usually the third or fourth.

The worked answer, the shape not your answer:

"Show me the line that produces your flip point." "It is the gross-margin row in the driver table, cell three. I rerun the whole model at 15.9% and the two cases meet — here. It is a rerun, not an interpolation, because the revolver draws in the low case and that is not linear."

Thirty seconds, it locates the number, and it discloses a subtlety the asker did not ask about. That last part is what separates a defence from an answer.

Part 4 — Being wrong, well

The strongest sentence available to you this week is some version of:

"That changes my answer, and here is how much."

It is not a concession. It is the demonstration that the number was never a belief you were defending, but a conclusion you would revise on evidence, which is the only kind of number worth having.

Three things that are graded as strength, not weakness:

  1. A prediction from Edition A that the model demolished, named plainly. You wrote that page before you knew anything. If every prediction survived, either the work was thin or the page was written late, and both are visible.
  2. A question you cannot answer in the room, followed by the answer in the memo. "I do not know, and I will find out" beats a confident invention, which the room can usually smell.
  3. A recommendation you decline to make. If your two ranges overlap so heavily that no honest ranking exists, say that, show why, and state what evidence would separate them. That is a real finding, and it is rarer and harder than picking one.

What earns nothing: pretending the sensitivity is small when your own table says it is not; a memo that reads as though the defence never happened; and a range so wide that it cannot be wrong.

Part 5 — Being the discussant

On the day you are not presenting you are doing the other half of the job, and it is graded.

A good question names one of their numbers and finds a tension with another one of their numbers. It is not a general question about valuation, and it is not a gotcha.
Weak "How did you pick your discount rate?" — answerable without having read anything
Weak "Did you consider a recession?" — invites a yes
Strong "Your flip point is a 15.9% margin, but your low case already runs 16.2%. Why does the low case not already flip the ranking?"
Strong "Both your companies use a 10% cost of equity, but one carries twice the leverage. Is the comparison fair?"

Both strong questions took one careful read of an exhibit. That read is the work, and it is why the question is submitted in advance.

Listening matters as much as asking. Your two-line record afterwards says what they answered and whether it moved you. A discussant who asks a sharp question and does not listen to the answer has done half the job.

Part 6 — What you submit afterwards, and why it is afterwards

The three capstone objects — the two-company model, the investment-committee memo, and the presentation video — are due after both defence days. That ordering is deliberate: feedback that cannot reach the artifact is decoration.

So the memo carries a short section that most memos do not: what the defence exposed, what you changed with the numbers before and after, and what you were asked to change and did not, with your reason. A grader reading that section learns more about your judgment than the rest of the memo combined.

The video is not a recording of your four minutes. It is the longer artifact the live slot had no room for: the model on screen as evidence, both sensitivity analyses, the validation, the walk from enterprise value to per-share value. The defence proves the judgment is yours; the video shows the work behind it. Its own page states its length and requirements.

This week's sources

Register: the source register.

Nothing new is pulled this week. Every figure you present was sourced in Weeks 5 and 6, and the only new requirement is that the market prices on your exhibit are dated within a week of your submission.

A number without a source is not an input — and this week, a number you cannot locate in your own notebook is not a number you can defend.

Before the session — do these

  1. Time the four minutes out loud, twice. Not in your head; it runs about 30% fast in your head.
  2. Open your notebook and find, in under ten seconds: the flip-point row, the terminal-value share, and the dated price for each company.
  3. Reread Edition A. Decide now what you will say about the prediction that did not survive.
  4. Submit your discussant question for the classmate you were assigned.
