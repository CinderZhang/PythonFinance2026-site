Lab 7 — The Analyst Deliverable and Its Defense

Register: DO. Teaching is in handout.md; conventions in rules.md.

You arrive with: everything. A security master, a panel and its catalog, verified metrics and a benchmark, a portfolio and its constraints, a backtested risk report, a dated valuation, and six weeks of findings you were not asked for.

Where this goes next: out of this course. This is the artefact you show an employer.

Step 1 — The fresh-kernel audit

Run everything from Week 1 to Week 6, start to finish, on a clean kernel, in order.

Record what breaks. Something will.

For each failure: what broke, why, and whether you fixed it or documented it. Both are acceptable answers this week. A known break that is documented is honest; a silent one is not.

If a piece of analysis cannot be rerun at all, say so in the deliverable and say what it would take. Do not quietly drop it — the reader needs to know it exists and cannot be reproduced.

Step 2 — Assemble the deliverable

Build the document using the handout's Part 2 structure:

  1. The finding — your conclusion, with range and confidence
  2. What it rests on — the two or three assumptions actually driving it
  3. What would change it — sensitivity, in statements about the world
  4. How it was built — data, method, choices
  5. What it cannot support — limitations, specifically
  6. Reproduce it — the source ledger and how to run the code

Lead with the finding. A reader who stops after the first page must still have your conclusion.

Step 3 — The consolidated limitations section

Pull together, from all six weeks, everything your work cannot support. Each entry:

  • the limitation
  • the evidence for it, with numbers
  • what the reader should therefore not conclude

Include, at minimum: your data-quality catalog's open issues, your risk model's measured breach ratio, the vintage of your valuation inputs, and the sensitivity of your conclusion to its driving assumption.

Never write "this analysis has limitations." Write the limitation.

Step 4 — The cold read

Give the deliverable to someone who has not seen your work. A classmate, a friend, anyone.

Record every question they ask. Then treat each question as a defect and fix the document so the next reader does not need to ask it.

Report: the questions you got, and what you changed. If they asked nothing, they did not read it carefully — get a better reader.

Step 5 — The reproducibility package

Assemble what a colleague needs:

  • the code, running on a fresh kernel
  • the source ledger, every input dated
  • data_quality_issues.md in its final state
  • a short README saying what this is, what question it answers, and how to run it
  • your conventions statement from Week 3

The test stands: someone who has never seen your work should be able to rebuild your numbers from this package alone.

Step 6 — The defense, on video

Record a video defending the single most load-bearing assumption in your work.

You will be given a fresh data point — something you have not seen, relevant to your company or your portfolio. Structure:

  1. State the assumption and why you made it
  2. Say what evidence supports it, and how strong that evidence is
  3. Engage with the fresh data point — what does it actually imply?
  4. Say whether it changes your conclusion, and by how much
  5. If it changes your conclusion, say so

Unaided. No script, no AI in the loop during the recording. The point is to find out whether the understanding is real, and both you and the grader will be able to tell.

"That changes my answer, and here is how" is the strongest thing you can say. It is not a concession. It is the demonstration.

Step 7 — Your work, your defense

The standing objection, one last time — and this time you write it about your own conclusion.

What is the strongest argument that your conclusion is wrong?

Not the easiest. The strongest. Then answer it, or concede it.

By now you know the pattern: conceding a good objection earns full marks. If, having written the strongest case against yourself, you find you no longer believe your own conclusion, say that in the deliverable. That outcome is rare, it is honest, and it is graded as a success.

Step 8 — What did you notice that I did not ask about?

The standing question, for the last time — and this time at term scale.

Across seven weeks, something recurred that no single lab asked about. A pattern in how data sources fail. A habit you developed. A question the course raised and never answered. Something you now think is wrong with how any of this is normally done.

Write it down with evidence.

Graded on genuineness and evidence. This is the last and least constrained thing you will write in this course. Use it.

If you want to do this differently

You may depart from the deliverable structure. A defended departure earns full marks. State what you did instead, why, and what you gave up.

If your honest conclusion is that the question you set out to answer cannot be answered responsibly with the data available, that is a legitimate deliverable — provided you show what you built, what you found, and precisely why it does not support a conclusion. Some of the most valuable work an analyst does is establishing that a number should not be produced.

Two sources, minimum — and where you have professional access, one of them is the professional source. This week that is whichever your figures came from — named, with IBES named wherever a forward estimate appears, for the ledger is the deliverable; an untraced figure is an unsupported one.

Pending access is neither an excuse nor a penalty. Document the attempt — source, route tried, date, barrier, and why that source would have been independent — and use a second free source instead. The attempt counts; the outcome never does.

After this course

Keep the package. Update it when something in it stops being true.

The specific numbers will age. The habits will not: source every input, date every figure, verify against something you did not write, and say plainly what your work cannot support.

That is the whole thing.
