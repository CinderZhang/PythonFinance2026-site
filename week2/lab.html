Lab 2 — Build a Panel and Find What Is Wrong With It

Register: DO. The teaching is in handout.md; conventions are in rules.md.

You arrive with: security_master.csv for one security, keyed on a permanent identifier, and source_log.md with every Week 1 adjudication. From the session you have three reproduced demonstrations — the absent failures, the SBNY label trap, and the 34-day filing lag — and the catalog format.

Where this goes next: Week 3 computes returns, risk, and a benchmark on the panel you hand it. It does not rebuild your panel. Every contamination you leave undocumented this week becomes a number nobody questions next week.

Step 1 — Choose your universe and say how you built the list

Extend your single security into a panel of 8 to 15 securities. The number is deliberately small; this is not a data engineering exercise.

Before you pull anything, write down:

  1. What universe are you covering, and why that one?
  2. Where did your list of names come from?
  3. Was that list constructed at the start of your sample period or at the end?

Question 3 is the one that matters. Answer it honestly before you see any data — it is much harder to be honest about it afterwards.

Step 2 — Find what is missing

Pull your panel. Then find what is not in it.

  • Which requested names returned no rows at all? Absence is not nullity — a name that

returned nothing will not appear in any null count.

  • For every name that did return data, check the coverage window against your intended

sample period. Which series start late? Which end early?

  • For any name whose coverage looks wrong, check what the ticker actually resolves to now —

exchange, name, market cap. The SBNY case is not rare.

Produce a table: requested name, rows returned, coverage window, and whether it spans your sample.

The shape, not your answer — from the taught example:

requested   rows   coverage                  spans sample?
FRC            0   —                         NO  - no rows returned
SIVBQ          0   —                         NO  - no rows returned
CS             0   —                         NO  - no rows returned
SBNY         502   2024-08-15 to 2026-08-20  NO  - begins after the event

Step 3 — Say how each problem stayed invisible

For every problem found in Step 2, write one line answering: what would have had to go wrong for me to notice this by accident?

This is the point of the exercise. A contamination that throws an error is a nuisance. A contamination that returns a clean dataframe is a career risk. Sort yours into the two piles and say which pile each is in.

Step 4 — Ask the survivorship question with a number

You cannot always fix survivorship. You can always size it.

Estimate how much your panel's headline statistic changes because of what is missing. You will not have the missing data — that is the whole problem — so you will have to bound it instead. Some ways to do that, any of which is acceptable if you defend it:

  • Compute your statistic on the names that span the full window, then on all names that

returned anything, and report both.

  • Find how many names you requested versus how many are usable, and state the attrition rate.
  • Use a public account of what happened to the missing names to bound their contribution.

State the number, state the method, and state what it does and does not establish. "I could not bound it, and here is what I would need to" is an acceptable and honest answer.

Step 5 — Put a knowability date on something

Pick one fundamental figure for one company in your panel. Using SEC EDGAR:

  1. Find the period end and the filing date.
  2. Compute the lag in days.
  3. State the date on which that figure first became usable in a time-sensitive analysis.
  4. Choose a lag policy for your panel — how many days after period end you will treat a

fundamental as knowable — and defend it against one alternative.

There is no correct lag policy. There is a defended one and an undefended one. A student who uses a conservative 45-day rule and says why is right; a student who uses period-end dating because it was on the row is wrong regardless of what number they get.

Step 6 — The catalog

Create data_quality_issues.md and enter every issue you found. Use the six fields from the handout: issue, severity, evidence, code reference, current state, proposed handling.

Rules:

  • Evidence is counts and percentages. "Some names are missing" is not evidence.

"3 of 12 requested names returned 0 rows (25%)" is.

  • Severity is your call and you must be able to defend it. Two analysts can rate the same

issue differently. Neither can decline to rate it.

  • At least one issue must be rated with reference to what you intend to do with the panel —

severity is not a property of the defect alone.

Then answer, in two sentences: is there a Blocker open on this panel, and if so, what does that forbid you from doing in Week 3?

If you have no Blocker, say why not. A panel with no Blocker is possible. A panel where nobody looked is not the same thing.

Step 7 — Your panel, your call

Steps 1–6 were the mechanism. This is the work.

You chose the universe in Step 1. Now defend it as an analyst would:

If a colleague told you this panel was not fit to answer your question, what would be their strongest argument — and what is your answer to it?

Write the strongest version of the objection, not the weakest. Then answer it, or concede it. Conceding a good objection is worth full marks. Straw-manning it is worth none.

Step 8 — What did you notice that I did not ask about?

The standing question.

Something in this week's work was not on the list above. A field that behaved oddly across names. A pattern in which securities went missing. Something about how a source handles an edge case. A question the handout raised and did not answer.

Write it down with your evidence.

Graded on genuineness and evidence, never on whether it was the right thing to notice. A hunch you chased and disproved is worth more than a safe observation. If you disagree with something taught this week and can support it, this is where that goes — and it is welcome.

If you want to do this differently

You may depart from the method above. A defended departure earns full marks. State what you did instead, why, and what you gave up.

If you think one of the three demonstrations proves something weaker than the handout claims, say so here with your reasoning. That is exactly the kind of departure this course wants.
