You arrive with: a portfolio, a risk report with its own failure rate on the face of it, a limitations section, and five weeks of habits about where numbers come from.

Now you value a single company. Everything narrows to one name and a small number of inputs — and every one of those inputs turns out to have a vintage.

Why this matters

Valuation is where analysts are hired and where they are caught out. Not because the models are hard — a DCF is arithmetic, and any AI will build you one in seconds — but because a valuation is only as good as the provenance of the numbers inside it, and almost nobody checks.

The question that separates a junior from a professional is not "what is your target price." It is "where did that number come from, and as of when?"

This week you will find that a blue-chip company reported two different figures for the same fiscal year, that both are correct, and that whichever your data vendor gave you depends on when they pulled it.

Part 1 — What is Apple's FY2017 operating cash flow?

It sounds like a fact. Ask the SEC:

import json, urllib.request
req = urllib.request.Request(
    "https://data.sec.gov/api/xbrl/companyconcept/CIK0000320193/"
    "us-gaap/NetCashProvidedByUsedInOperatingActivities.json",
    headers={"User-Agent": "your.name@purdue.edu"})
d = json.load(urllib.request.urlopen(req))

for f in d["units"]["USD"]:
    if f.get("start") == "2016-09-25" and f.get("end") == "2017-09-30":
        print(f["form"], "filed", f["filed"], f"{f['val']:>18,}")
10-K filed 2017-11-03      63,598,000,000
10-K filed 2018-11-05      64,225,000,000
10-K filed 2019-10-31      64,225,000,000

The same fiscal period. The same company. The same line item. Two different numbers.

As first reported in the FY2017 annual report: $63.598 billion. As it appears in the FY2018 annual report a year later, shown as the prior-year comparative: $64.225 billion.

A difference of $627 million — about 1% — on one of the most scrutinised financial statements in the world.

Neither is an error

This is not a scandal and nobody did anything wrong. Figures get revised: accounting standards change and require retrospective restatement, classifications are refined, discontinued operations are reclassified, errors are corrected.

What matters is that "Apple's FY2017 operating cash flow" is not a single fact. It is a fact with a vintage, and the number you get depends on which filing you read.

Now ask the question that should be uncomfortable: your data vendor gave you one of these. Do you know which? Do you know when they pulled it? Would you be able to reproduce their figure in a year?

Part 2 — Three different things, routinely confused

TermMeansUse it for
As first reportedthe figure in the original filing for that periodunderstanding what the market saw at the time
As-reported (current)the figure as it stands now, restatements includedcurrent-state analysis, comparability across years
Point-in-timethe figure as known on a specified dateanything time-sensitive, especially backtests

Week 2 taught you that a fundamental has a knowability date. This week adds the second dimension: it also has a value that changes. A figure has both a date it became knowable and a date it was last revised, and a rigorous analysis states both.

The practical consequence: a backtest that ranks companies on operating cash flow, using data pulled today, is ranking them on numbers that in many cases did not exist in that form at the time. The bias is subtle and it favours whichever companies were later revised upward.

Part 3 — The year that had fifty-three weeks

Apple does not use a calendar year. Its fiscal year ends on the last Saturday of September, so most years are exactly 52 weeks — and occasionally one has to be 53 to keep the calendar aligned.

Apple annual periods, from the 10-K filings

2017-10-01 -> 2018-09-29    364 days = 52.00 weeks
2018-09-30 -> 2019-09-28    364 days = 52.00 weeks
2019-09-29 -> 2020-09-26    364 days = 52.00 weeks
2020-09-27 -> 2021-09-25    364 days = 52.00 weeks
2021-09-26 -> 2022-09-24    364 days = 52.00 weeks
2022-09-25 -> 2023-09-30    371 days = 53.00 weeks   <-- an extra week
2023-10-01 -> 2024-09-28    364 days = 52.00 weeks
2024-09-29 -> 2025-09-27    364 days = 52.00 weeks

FY2023 contained an extra week. Roughly 1.9% more selling days than the years either side of it.

So when you compute FY2023 revenue growth over FY2022, part of that growth is a week. And when you compute FY2024 growth over FY2023, part of that decline is the same week going away. An analyst who reports either number without mentioning it has introduced a real distortion into a growth series, and it will look exactly like a business trend.

52/53-week fiscal calendars are common — retailers use them almost universally. Companies with different fiscal year-ends cannot be compared quarter-to-quarter without alignment. Neither problem announces itself.

The rule this gives you. Before comparing any two periods, check they are the same length and cover the same season. This takes thirty seconds and it is skipped constantly.

Part 4 — Why two platforms give you different numbers

You now have enough to predict the answer. When Capital IQ, LSEG, Compustat, and the filings themselves disagree about one company's revenue, it is almost always one of these:

CauseWhat is happening
Vintageone pulled before a restatement, one after
Fiscal alignmentone calendarised the fiscal year, one did not
Standardisationplatforms map filings into their own schema, and make judgment calls doing it
Classificationdiscontinued operations, one-offs, and segment definitions treated differently
GAAP vs non-GAAPone uses the company's adjusted figures, one uses the statutory ones

Standardisation is the one students underestimate. Compustat does not simply copy the filings — it maps them onto a consistent schema so that thousands of companies can be compared. That mapping is the product, and it involves decisions. Two vendors making different reasonable decisions produce different numbers from identical filings, and neither is wrong.

This is the professional-data point of the week, and it inverts a natural assumption. You might expect the expensive platform to be more faithful to the filing. Often it is deliberately less faithful, because comparability across companies is worth more than fidelity to any one of them. Knowing which you need is the skill.

Part 5 — GAAP, non-GAAP, and the adjustment nobody defines

Most companies report both statutory (GAAP) figures and their own adjusted figures. Adjusted numbers exclude things management considers unrepresentative — restructuring, acquisition costs, share-based compensation, impairments.

Sometimes that is genuinely informative. Sometimes it is the same "one-off" charge appearing for six consecutive years.

Three rules that will keep you out of trouble:

  1. Non-GAAP is not standardised. Two companies' "adjusted EBITDA" are not comparable

unless you check both reconciliations.

  1. The reconciliation is required and is published. Read it. It tells you exactly what was

excluded.

  1. A recurring adjustment is not an adjustment. If it appears every year, it is a cost of

doing business.

State which basis you used. A valuation built on adjusted figures and presented without saying so is misleading even when every number in it is accurate.

Part 6 — The source ledger

Everything above resolves into one artefact: for every input in your valuation, you record where it came from and as of when.

Field
Inputwhat it is, in your model
Valuethe number you used
Sourcefiling, platform, or your own calculation
As-of datethe date the figure was published or pulled
BasisGAAP or non-GAAP; fiscal or calendar
Noteanything that would surprise a reader

The shape, not your answer — one filled row:

Input: FY2017 operating cash flow Value: $63.598bn Source: Apple 10-K for FY2017, filed 2017-11-03, via SEC EDGAR XBRL As-of date: as first reported, 2017-11-03 Basis: GAAP, fiscal year (52/53-week, ends last Saturday of September) Note: Revised to $64.225bn in the FY2018 10-K. I use the as-first-reported figure because this analysis reconstructs what was knowable at the time. A current-state analysis should use $64.225bn instead.

That note is the difference between an analyst and a spreadsheet. It does not just record the number — it records that there is a choice, which one was made, and when the other would be right.

The rule this gives you. A valuation is a chain of sourced inputs. Any link you cannot date is a link you cannot defend, and it is the one you will be asked about.

Part 7 — The model is the easy part

You can build a DCF in twenty minutes and AI can build one in twenty seconds. That is not where the value is, and it is not where you will be tested.

Where valuations actually go wrong:

  • The terminal value, which is usually most of the answer and rests on a growth rate somebody

chose

  • A discount rate assembled from components each carrying their own estimation error — and you

have now spent a week on what estimation error does

  • Growth assumptions extrapolated from a series containing a 53-week year
  • Comparables selected, consciously or not, to support a conclusion already reached
  • Inputs of unknown vintage

Notice that only one of those is arithmetic. Spend your time on the inputs and the assumptions, because that is where the answer actually comes from — and be honest about how wide the range is. A single point estimate presented without a range is a claim to precision you do not have.

A number without a source is not an input. Anything can produce a plausible figure — a spreadsheet, a summary, a half-remembered fact, a model. Plausible is cheap.

What makes a figure usable in a valuation is that you can name the filing it came from and the date it was published, and that someone else could retrieve the same figure from the same place. Everything in your ledger meets that standard or it is marked as not meeting it.

Two sources, minimum — and where you have professional access, one of them is the professional source. This week that is Compustat, beside SEC EDGAR as-filed — and IBES for any forward growth, for standardisation is a choice, not a fact, and no filing contains a forward estimate.

Pending access is neither an excuse nor a penalty. Document the attempt — source, route tried, date, barrier, and why that source would have been independent — and use a second free source instead. The attempt counts; the outcome never does.
