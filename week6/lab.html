Week 6 — Lab

Lab 6 — A Valuation You Can Date

Register: DO. Teaching is in handout.md; conventions in rules.md.

You arrive with: a company you chose, a risk report with its own failure rate on it, a limitations section, and five weeks of habits about provenance. From the session you have the Apple restatement, the 53-week year, and the source ledger format.

Where this goes next: Week 7 assembles everything into one deliverable and you defend it on video. This valuation is its centrepiece, and the source ledger is what makes it reproducible by someone else.

---

Step 1 — Find a revision in your own company

Run the XBRL company-concept query on your company for at least two line items.

Look for any period reported with more than one value across filings. Report what you find.

If you find nothing, that is a legitimate result — report it as "no revisions found across N filings for these concepts," and say which concepts you checked. A negative result you actually looked for is worth full marks. A negative result you assumed is worth none.

Step 2 — Establish the fiscal calendar

For your company, report:

  • fiscal year end, and whether it is a calendar year
  • period lengths for the last five to eight annual periods, in days and weeks
  • any 53-week year, or any change of fiscal year end

Then state: does any growth rate you intend to compute span a period of unequal length? If so, say what you will do about it.

Step 3 — Two sources, one number

Pick one fundamental figure. Get it from two sources — the filings themselves plus one other, or two platforms if you have access.

Report both, the difference, and your diagnosis using the handout's Part 4 table: vintage, fiscal alignment, standardisation, classification, or GAAP/non-GAAP.

You have done this exercise before, in Week 1, on prices. It is the same skill on harder data.

Step 4 — Build the valuation

Value your company. DCF, multiples, or both — your choice, stated and justified.

Requirements:

  • Every input appears in the source ledger with its as-of date and basis
  • The discount rate's components are each sourced or explicitly assumed
  • Terminal value assumptions are stated separately and prominently
  • Report a range, not a point. A single number implies a precision you do not have

State what fraction of your valuation comes from the terminal value. If it is most of it — it usually is — say so plainly.

Step 5 — The source ledger

Complete the ledger for every input: input, value, source, as-of date, basis, note.

The test: someone who has never seen your work should be able to rebuild your valuation from the ledger alone. That is also the Week 7 standard, so getting it right now saves you later.

Any input you cannot date goes in the ledger marked as undated, with a note saying what you would need. Do not quietly leave it out.

Step 6 — Sensitivity: what would have to be true

Pick the two assumptions your valuation is most sensitive to. For each:

  • How much does the valuation move over a plausible range?
  • What would have to be true about the business for the high end to be right? For the low

end?

The second question is the one that matters. A sensitivity table is arithmetic. Translating it into statements about the world is analysis, and it is what someone reading your work actually needs.

Step 7 — Your company, your judgment

You chose the company and every assumption in the model.

A colleague says your valuation is really just your terminal growth assumption wearing a spreadsheet, and that with a different assumption — equally defensible — you would get a materially different answer.

They are very likely right about the arithmetic. Write the strongest version of their case, including the number that supports it. Then answer it or concede it.

Conceding earns full marks. So does a good answer about why your assumption is better supported than the alternative. What earns nothing is pretending the sensitivity is small when your own Step 6 table says it is not.

Step 8 — What did you notice that I did not ask about?

The standing question.

Something in your company's filings or data was odd. A line item that moved without explanation. A segment definition that changed. A disclosure that answered a question nobody asked. A difference between what management emphasised and what the statements showed.

Write it down with evidence.

Graded on genuineness and evidence, never on whether it was the right thing to notice. This is the week where the finding slot most often produces something genuinely interesting, because you are reading one company closely rather than a panel broadly.

If you want to do this differently

You may depart from the method above — a different valuation approach, a different treatment of the terminal value, a decision not to produce a point estimate at all. A defended departure earns full marks. State what you did instead, why, and what you gave up.

A well-argued "this company cannot be responsibly valued with the information available to me, and here is why" is a legitimate and sometimes excellent answer.

Two sources, minimum — and where you have professional access, one of them is the professional source. This week that is Compustat, beside SEC EDGAR as-filed — and IBES for any forward growth, for standardisation is a choice, not a fact, and no filing contains a forward estimate.

Pending access is neither an excuse nor a penalty. Document the attempt — source, route tried, date, barrier, and why that source would have been independent — and use a second free source instead. The attempt counts; the outcome never does.
