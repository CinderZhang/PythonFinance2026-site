Both checkpoints are scored on submitting the state, not on reaching the end. A model that fails its check block, submitted with one line naming which check failed and why you think it did, scores the same as one that passes. A blank scores nothing. That is deliberate: a named failure is evidence of work and a green tick from an agent is not.

Data and tools this week

Professional sources, not Yahoo Finance. Every filed line, price and estimate in this lab, in Exercise 4 and in the capstone comes from a professional source or from the filing itself: Compustat and CRSP on WRDS, Capital IQ, LSEG Workspace, or the Financial Datasets API, beside SEC EDGAR as filed. Yahoo Finance and yfinance are not accepted sources for any input here: not for the filed lines, not for the market price your range is read against, not for the beta. Where you have professional access, use it. Where it is still pending, document the attempt and use the filing; the attempt counts, the outcome never does. A free consumer feed is not an attempt.

Python does the work. The pull, the calendar check, the pro-forma, the check block, the discounted cash flow, the sensitivity and the exhibit all live in your notebook and rerun from a fresh kernel. Hand-typed numbers are limited to the four filing reads from prework Part B, each with a comment naming the statement it came from. A spreadsheet may be a scratchpad; it is not the model, and a figure that exists only in a spreadsheet cell has no source.

Part 1 — Tuesday (Checkpoint 9)

Step 0 — Where everyone is, out loud (10 min)

The poll said who reproduced ABG. Find a partner on the other side of that line. Two minutes each: the first line where your number diverged, or, if you did not start, the step that stopped you.

Step 1 — The model, built once, together (35 min)

The instructor builds ABG's skeleton at the front. Type it as it goes. Do not watch. Do not paste it from an agent. The order matters more than the arithmetic, and the order is the thing an agent gets wrong:

  1. the income statement down to net income
  2. the balance sheet items that follow from drivers: inventory, floor plan, PP&E, debt after the paydown, equity after net income and the buyback
  3. free cash flow to equity
  4. cash, last, from opening cash plus that flow less the buyback, with the revolver drawn only if cash would fall below the floor
  5. the check block: the balance identity, the cash tie, the cash floor
  6. the valuation cell, which refuses to run if the checks fail or if terminal growth is not below the discount rate

Known answer: $291.75 a share, 79.8% terminal. You leave this step with a model that produces it. If yours does not, that is what the rest of the session's help is for; say so now rather than at 4:15.

Step 2 — Swap in company A (25 min)

Your ABG notebook has two halves. The company half holds the filed lines and the nineteen assumptions. The method half holds the projection, the checks and the discounted cash flow, and it does not know what company it is running. Separate them if they are not already separate: that separation is the whole reason company B will be cheap on Thursday.

Now replace the company half with company A, from prework Part C.

Two things will not carry over, and both are the point:

  • The drivers are not ABG's. Your company does not finance inventory with floor-plan notes. Work out its own drivers from its own history: the growth rate, the margin, the cost ratio, the tax rate, the reinvestment. Label each one history, guidance or judgment.
  • The known answer is not $291.75. It is your company's own last filed year. Before projecting anything, run your pipeline on the year that already happened and print revenue, operating income and net income beside the filed figures. If the rebuilt year does not reproduce the filed year, stop and fix that; every projected year is wrong in the same way until you do.

Step 3 — Edition A, on paper, no AI (15 min)

Close the laptop. This page is written in the room, by hand or in a plain file with no agent open, and it is the only writing in this course produced without AI. It is one page, and it takes the form:

  • Company A, one paragraph: what this business does, and the single driver you expect its value to turn on.
  • Company B, one paragraph: the same.
  • Your prediction, three lines: which one you expect to be worth more relative to its market price, roughly how wide you expect each range to be, and the assumption you expect to matter most.

You are predicting before the model answers. Most of these predictions will be wrong, and that is the point: the capstone memo ends by comparing this page with what the model produced and saying what changed. A prediction written afterwards is worth nothing, and cannot be written afterwards, because this one is timestamped in the room.

Part 2 — Thursday (Checkpoint 10)

Step 4 — Company B, through the same pipeline (25 min)

Swap the company half again. If Tuesday's separation was clean this is minutes, not an hour, and that is the lesson: one pipeline, run twice. If it is not minutes, the separation was not clean, and fixing it now is worth more than finishing.

Same known-answer check: company B's last filed year, reproduced, before anything is projected.

Step 5 — One exhibit, both companies (20 min)

Both ranges on one picture, each against its own dated market price, with the date and source of each price written down. The price comes from a professional source, per the rule at the top of this page: CRSP or the Compustat security file on WRDS, Capital IQ, or the Financial Datasets price endpoint, pulled in code.

Then the number a committee actually acts on: for each company, the gap between your range and the price, as a percentage. Ranges in dollars cannot be compared across two companies. Gaps can.

Check the comparison is fair before you read it. Same horizon, same terminal convention, discount rates built from the same components with only the beta and the leverage differing. If they differ in any other way, you are ranking your own method, not the businesses.

Step 6 — The decision, the flip point, the triggers (25 min)

This is the graded core of the week, and the part no agent can produce, because it requires running your model.

  1. The recommendation, one sentence: which company gets the capital, and the condition under which that holds.
  2. The flip point: the single assumption that, moved alone, reverses the ranking, and the value at which it reverses. Find it by rerunning, not by arithmetic on the sensitivity table. Say whether that value is inside or outside the company's own history.
  3. Two triggers, one per company, each with all three parts from handout Part 10: an observable you could look up, a threshold with a direction, and a review date.

The shape, not your answer: "We recommend A over B. The ranking turns on A's gross margin, held at 17.05%. At 15.9% the cases meet and B ranks higher. A's margin has not been below 16.4% in ten years, which is why the recommendation holds. Trigger: if same-store gross margin prints below 16.4% in the Q1 10-Q, due late April, we re-rank."

Step 7 — The peer read (15 min)

Swap your exhibit and your three decision lines with one other person. You each write three defects on the other's, in writing, and hand them back. Defects, not compliments, and not fixes: name what is missing, what is unsupported, or what a committee would ask first.

You are graded on the note you receive being real, and on nothing about the other person's work. Their grade is theirs.

Submit now — Checkpoint 10. Your exhibit, your three decision lines from Step 6, the peer note you received, and your AI disclosure. Brightspace: Lab 6 — Capstone Studio: Two Companies and the Decision — Thursday record (CP10). Before you leave the room.

The floor: both companies' last filed years reproduced, one exhibit with both ranges against dated prices, and a flip point with a number on it. The full sensitivity on both companies is outside the room.

Step 9 — What did you notice that I did not ask about?

The standing question, not collected. Something in one of your companies behaved unexpectedly: a line that moved without explanation, a tag that went empty, a segment that was redefined, a share count that fell faster than you expected. Write it down with evidence. The capstone memo runs on this habit.

If you want to do this differently

You may depart from the method above. A defended departure earns full marks. State what you did instead, why, and what you gave up.

Two departures are welcome: discount free cash flow to the firm at a weighted cost of capital and bridge to equity with dated net debt, stating both rates, provided you do it for both companies so the comparison stays fair; or run a multiples check beside the discounted cash flow, the way Kaplan and Ruback found the two together did better than either.

Two sources, minimum, and where you have professional access, one of them is the professional source. This week that is Compustat beside SEC EDGAR as filed, which is where the AI-risk check comes from; Capital IQ, LSEG or the Financial Datasets API stand in for Compustat if that is what you have. Yahoo Finance is never one of the two. Pending access is neither an excuse nor a penalty. Document the attempt and use the filing. The attempt counts; the outcome never does.
