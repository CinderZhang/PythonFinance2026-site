Lab 3 — Metrics, Verified, Against a Benchmark You Chose

Register: DO. Teaching is in handout.md; conventions in rules.md.

You arrive with: your panel, a permanent identifier on every row, data_quality_issues.md with severities you assigned, and a statement of what your panel cannot be used for. From the session you have the SPY/RSP comparison, the annualization convention gap, and the idea that an index is a membership dataset.

Where this goes next: Week 4 builds portfolios from the return and covariance estimates you produce here. Every convention you leave unstated becomes a silent assumption inside an optimizer.

---

Step 1 — State your conventions before you compute

Write down, before running anything:

  • Return type: simple or log, and why
  • Annualization basis: 252, 365, or something else, and why
  • Risk-free rate: what you will use, or that you are using zero and saying so
  • Window: exact start and end, and why that window

Four lines. Two minutes now, and it saves an argument later. Every number you report for the rest of the course inherits these.

Step 2 — Compute the metrics

For your panel, compute per security and for an equal-weighted portfolio of it:

  • total return over the window
  • annualized return
  • annualized volatility
  • Sharpe ratio, with your stated risk-free assumption

Then compute annualized volatility a second way, using a different annualization basis, and report both. State which one you will use for the rest of the course, and why.

Step 3 — Verify against something you did not write

Pick at least two of your computed numbers and check them against an independent source that did not come from your code — an issuer factsheet, an index provider, a vendor's computed metric.

For each, record: your number, their number, the gap, and your explanation. Work the handout's Part 4 list in order — convention, then window, then computation.

A verification that fails to match, is investigated, and is honestly reported as unresolved is a complete answer. "Verified" with nothing behind it is not.

Step 4 — Choose a benchmark, and say what it is made of

Choose a benchmark for your panel. Then answer:

  1. What is it, and what rules define its membership?
  2. Why is it the right comparison for your panel specifically?
  3. What is the obvious alternative, and what would change if you used it instead?

For question 3, compute it. Report your panel's excess return against both benchmarks. The SPY/RSP example exists so you know how large that difference can get.

If you cannot obtain a point-in-time membership file for your benchmark, say so, and say what it prevents you from claiming.

Step 5 — Say what your catalog allows you to claim

Reread data_quality_issues.md. Then write a short section titled "What these numbers can and cannot support."

Name each open issue and state, concretely, what it forbids. Then choose one of the handout's three options for each — fix the input, bound the damage, or restrict the claim — and do it.

If a Blocker is still open, no headline result is reported this week. Say that plainly and report what you can.

Step 6 — Your benchmark, your defence

You chose the benchmark in Step 4. Now defend it.

A colleague says your benchmark flatters your panel and you should have used the other one. What is their strongest argument, and what is your answer?

Write the strongest version of their case, including any number that supports it. Then answer it or concede it. Conceding a good objection earns full marks.

Step 7 — What did you notice that I did not ask about?

The standing question.

Something in this week's computation behaved in a way the steps did not anticipate. A metric that moved more than you expected when a convention changed. A security whose numbers look wrong next to its peers. A relationship you did not expect in your panel.

Write it down with evidence.

Graded on genuineness and evidence, never on whether it was the right thing to notice. If you think one of this week's claims is overstated and can support that, this is where it goes.

If you want to do this differently

You may depart from the method above — a different metric set, a different verification strategy, a benchmark nobody would expect. A defended departure earns full marks. State what you did instead, why, and what you gave up.

Two sources, minimum — and where you have professional access, one of them is the professional source. This week that is WRDS / CRSP, for point-in-time index membership, so your benchmark carries no look-ahead.

Pending access is neither an excuse nor a penalty. Document the attempt — source, route tried, date, barrier, and why that source would have been independent — and use a second free source instead. The attempt counts; the outcome never does.
