Why this matters

A portfolio manager looks at a position that returned 22% a year for a decade and asks one question: did it earn its risk? Three numbers answer it, and every desk that reports performance produces all three.

The Sharpe ratio says what the position paid per unit of risk taken. Jensen's alpha says what it paid after the market's share is removed. The risk decomposition says how much of the risk was the market's and how much was the position's own. A junior analyst who reports 22% has reported a number. One who reports the three, with the conventions behind them, has reported a result.

The three come from one regression and one convention. This week you learn both, on eight companies whose numbers are printed below so you can check your own.

The paper

This week is anchored on Sharpe (1964), "Capital Asset Prices: A Theory of Market Equilibrium under Conditions of Risk," Journal of Finance 19(3), 425–442. It is the paper the Capital Asset Pricing Model comes from, and its one equation is the reason the three numbers above exist:

E[r_i] − r_f  =  β_i · ( E[r_m] − r_f )

In words: the expected excess return on any asset is its beta times the expected excess return on the market. Nothing else is rewarded. The part of an asset's risk that is not the market's, Sharpe argued, can be diversified away, so the market does not pay for it.

Jensen (1968), "The Performance of Mutual Funds in the Period 1945–1964," Journal of Finance 23(2), 389–416, turned that equation into a regression you can run on data and named the intercept. The digits below are Jensen's procedure on a modern panel.

Read: Sharpe's introduction and the section on equilibrium in the capital market, through the figure with the capital market line, about eight pages. Jensen's introduction and the section that defines the regression, about six pages. Both are on JSTOR through Purdue Libraries. Start here: https://www.lib.purdue.edu → Databases → JSTOR. Sharpe's DOI is 10.1111/j.1540-6261.1964.tb02865.x; Jensen's is 10.1111/j.1540-6261.1968.tb00815.x.

What the paper does not say. It does not say that a positive intercept is skill. It says that, if the model is right, the intercept should be zero. Everything you do with alpha this week sits on that "if."

Textbook. The free-lane counterpart, with code, is in the DRIVER textbook: Session 5 — Portfolio Metrics for the Sharpe ratio, beta, and alpha, and Session 1 — Total Return Analysis for why an adjusted close is a total-return proxy. Read the paper for the idea and the textbook for the code.

Part 1 — Excess returns first, and the risk-free rate is data

Every number this week is computed on excess returns: the security's return minus the one-month Treasury bill rate in the same month. Not the raw return. The raw return includes what you would have earned for doing nothing.

The risk-free series is not a constant you type in. It is a column in the Ken French Data Library, the free reference source for factor data, and it comes bundled with the market factor you will need in Part 3.

Get it:

  1. Go to https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html
  2. Under U.S. Research Returns Data, find Fama/French 3 Factors and click CSV. The file is F-F_Research_Data_Factors_CSV.zip.
  3. Unzip. The CSV has a four-line preamble, then a header ,Mkt-RF,SMB,HML,RF, then one row per month from 192607. Values are in percent. The monthly block ends where a blank line and the word "Annual" begin.

The last three monthly rows on the day this was written:

202605,   4.91,  -1.59,  -2.31,   0.31
202606,  -1.07,   3.39,   3.58,   0.29
202607,  -0.60,  -1.96,   2.11,   0.33

Mkt-RF is the return on the whole US equity market already in excess of the bill rate. RF is the bill rate itself. If you subtract RF from Mkt-RF, you have subtracted it twice. That is the single most common error in student factor regressions.

Over the worked window, July 2015 to June 2025, the mean monthly bill rate was 0.1571%, about 1.88% a year. In 2015 it was zero. In 2025 it was 0.34% a month. A Sharpe ratio that ignores it is wrong by an amount that changes over time.

Part 2 — The Sharpe ratio, stated properly

The Sharpe ratio is the mean excess return divided by the standard deviation of excess return, scaled to a year:

Sharpe  =  mean( r − r_f ) / sd( r − r_f )  ×  √12         [monthly data]

For Apple, July 2015 to June 2025, 120 months:

mean monthly excess  =  1.8291%
sd monthly excess    =  8.0395%   (sample sd, ddof = 1)
Sharpe               =  1.8291 / 8.0395 × √12  =  0.788

That is the whole computation. What makes it a professional number is stating the three choices inside it, because each one moves the answer.

The three choices.

  1. Excess or raw. Above, excess. Using raw returns with the risk-free rate silently set to zero gives Apple 0.857 instead of 0.788. For Coca-Cola it is 0.653 instead of 0.533. The gap is the bill rate, and the bill rate was zero in 2015 and 4% in 2024, so the error is not constant across windows.
  2. Arithmetic or geometric mean. Above, the arithmetic mean of monthly returns, annualized by ×12. The alternative is the compound annual growth rate, CAGR: the single yearly rate that, compounded, turns the starting dollar into the ending dollar. One dollar in Apple in July 2015 was $7.30 in June 2025, so
CAGR  =  ( 7.3035 ) ^ (12 / 120)  −  1   =   22.00% per year

The arithmetic mean of the same 120 months, ×12, is 23.83%. The two are different questions. CAGR answers "what did I actually earn per year," compounding included. The arithmetic mean answers "what did a typical month pay," which is what a Sharpe ratio is built on, because volatility is measured around that mean. Over one horizon the arithmetic mean is never below the geometric one, and volatility drives them apart: two annual returns of +50% and −50% average to zero and compound to −13.4% a year, because $1 became $0.75. Across horizons the order can flip, because ×12 ignores compounding inside the year: Microsoft's arithmetic 28.04% sits below its 29.23% CAGR. Dividing a CAGR by a volatility mixes the two and is a common shortcut; it is not a Sharpe ratio. Microsoft's Sharpe is 1.248; its CAGR over volatility is 1.396. 3. Sample or population standard deviation. pandas uses the sample version by default (ddof=1); numpy uses the population version (ddof=0). On 120 months the difference is 0.4% of the answer, invisible here and a real discrepancy source on short windows.

The rule this gives you. A Sharpe ratio is reported with its three choices. "Sharpe 0.79" is not a result. "Sharpe 0.79, monthly excess returns over one-month bills, arithmetic mean, sample sd, ×√12, 2015-07 to 2025-06" is.

Part 3 — Jensen's alpha: one regression

Jensen's procedure is an ordinary least squares regression of the security's excess return on the market's excess return:

r_i − r_f  =  α_i  +  β_i · ( r_m − r_f )  +  ε_i

The slope β is the security's sensitivity to the market. The intercept α is the average excess return the security earned that the market does not account for. The residual ε is what is left each month.

For Apple on the French market factor, 120 months:

α  =  0.0070 per month   →  8.41% per year     t = 1.23
β  =  1.1335                                   t = 9.41
R² =  0.429
residual sd  =  6.10% per month   →  21.1% per year

Read that. Apple earned 0.70% a month more than a 1.13-beta position in the market would have. The t-statistic says the estimate is 1.23 standard errors from zero. A 95% interval on the monthly alpha runs from −0.43% to +1.83%. It includes zero. You may not call Apple's alpha "positive" with that interval; you may call it "8.4% a year, not distinguishable from zero on 120 months."

Three things to notice.

Every beta has a t-statistic near or above 4. Betas are estimated precisely on ten years of monthly data. Alphas are not: one of eight clears 2. That asymmetry is permanent. Expected returns are hard to estimate; sensitivities are not.

The R² column is not fit quality in the usual sense. It is the fraction of the security's variance that the market explains. Procter & Gamble's 0.119 means 88% of its month-to-month movement had nothing to do with the market. That number is Part 4.

The equal-weight panel has a t(α) of 2.71 while seven of its eight members do not clear 2. Nothing was added. Idiosyncratic noise was averaged out, and the standard error fell with it. This is diversification seen from the alpha side.

The code, in full, because Week 4 inherits it:

import statsmodels.api as sm
X = sm.add_constant(df["Mkt-RF"])          # column of ones + market excess return
m = sm.OLS(df["AAPL"] - df["RF"], X).fit()  # excess return on the left
alpha_m, beta = m.params["const"], m.params["Mkt-RF"]
print(alpha_m*12, m.tvalues["const"], beta, m.rsquared, (m.mse_resid**0.5)*12**0.5)

df holds monthly simple returns in decimals with the French columns already divided by 100 and aligned on year-month. The alignment is the part AI-written code gets wrong: French rows are keyed 202506, yfinance rows are keyed by the last trading day. Convert both to a monthly period before joining, and assert the row count you expect. Here it is 120.

Part 4 — Risk decomposition, and why diversification works

The regression splits each month's excess return into a market part and a residual. Because the residual is uncorrelated with the market by construction, the variances add:

total variance  =  β² · var(r_m − r_f)  +  var(ε)
                   systematic            idiosyncratic

For Apple, in monthly variance units:

β² · var_m  =  1.1335² × 0.002157  =  0.002771
var(ε)      =                          0.003723
sum         =                          0.006494     total variance, measured directly: 0.006463

The 0.5% gap is a degrees-of-freedom difference: the regression's residual variance is divided by n − 2 and the total by n − 1. Divide the residual sum of squares by n − 1 instead and the identity is exact; the tables keep the regression's n − 2 figure because that is what the software reports, so expect the check to close within about 1%, not to the last digit. Annualized and in standard-deviation units, the same split reads: total 27.9%, systematic 18.2%, idiosyncratic 21.1%, and √(18.2² + 21.1²) = 27.9. Systematic volatility is |β| times market volatility; the absolute value matters only for a negative-beta security, and none of these eight is one.

Read the last row against the eight above it. Every single name carries idiosyncratic volatility between 14% and 24% a year. The equal-weight portfolio of the same eight carries 6.7%. The systematic part did not shrink; it is the weighted average of the betas, 0.721, times the market's 16.1%. The idiosyncratic part collapsed, because eight residuals that are mostly unrelated to each other average toward zero.

That is Sharpe's argument in a table. The market does not pay for idiosyncratic risk because an investor can remove most of it by holding eight names instead of one. Whatever is left, 6.7% here, is the part your panel's construction chose to keep.

The covariance matrix Week 4 needs. Week 4 feeds an optimizer with two inputs: the mean of each security's excess return and the covariance matrix of those excess returns. The second is the sample covariance, annualized, and for eight names it holds 36 distinct numbers, each estimated from the same 120 months. The 3×3 corner:

            AAPL    MSFT     JNJ
AAPL      0.0776  0.0346  0.0148
MSFT      0.0346  0.0439  0.0103
JNJ       0.0148  0.0103  0.0260

The diagonal is the variance you already have: 0.0776 is 27.9% squared. The off-diagonal is what the optimizer acts on, and 0.0346 says Apple and Microsoft move together at a correlation of 0.59. Save the matrix with the return series. Week 4 begins by asking how much you should trust those 36 numbers.

Part 5 — Which market? Alpha depends on the regressor

Jensen regressed on "the market." There is no such series. There is French's Mkt-RF, the value-weighted return on every listed US stock. There is SPY, the investable cap-weighted S&P 500. There is RSP, the same 500 companies weighted equally. Each is a defensible market, and each gives a different alpha:

Alpha per year vs French Mkt-RF vs SPY vs RSP
AAPL 8.41% 7.58% 13.47%
MSFT 15.22% 14.35% 19.68%
KO 3.31% 2.47% 3.83%
XOM −1.49% −2.00% −0.66%
Equal-weight panel 5.89% 5.23% 8.04%

Same panel. Same 120 months. The equal-weight panel's alpha is 5.2% against SPY and 8.0% against RSP. Alpha is the panel's mean excess return minus beta times the benchmark's mean excess return, so a benchmark with a lower mean, RSP returned 3.1 percentage points a year less than SPY over this decade, leaves more for the intercept, and every name looks better against it.

Why RSP returned less is worth one paragraph. SPY weights each company by market capitalization; RSP holds the same companies at equal weight and rebalances back to equal weight every quarter. Over this decade the largest companies outperformed, so the cap-weighted fund won. RSP also charges 0.20% a year against SPY's 0.09%, and its quarterly rebalancing has a cost. The two funds differ in construction, in fees, and in implementation, and the 3.1-point gap is all three together. "Same companies, different weights" is the headline; it is not the whole story.

The rule this gives you. A benchmark is a choice, and the choice moves alpha by more than most alphas are worth. Name it, say what it is made of, and compute the obvious alternative so you know how large the gap is before a colleague computes it for you.

Part 6 — The equal-weight portfolio is also a convention

"Equal-weighted portfolio of my panel" hides a choice. Do you rebalance back to equal weight every month, or buy equal amounts once and let the weights drift?

Same eight names, same window:

Monthly-rebalanced 1/8      CAGR 16.66%   vol 13.38%   Sharpe 1.083   $1 → $4.67
Buy-and-hold 1/8 at start   CAGR 17.52%   vol 14.14%   Sharpe 1.086   $1 → $5.02

Buy-and-hold won by 0.9 points a year here because it let the winners run and the winners kept winning. Over a mean-reverting decade it goes the other way. Neither is the correct equal-weight return. State which one you computed. The regression tables above use the monthly-rebalanced version, because that is what df[NAMES].mean(axis=1) computes, and that is what most students will write without noticing they chose.

Part 7 — Verify against something you did not write

Beta is the one number this week that every vendor publishes. The Yahoo Finance quote page for any ticker shows Beta (5Y Monthly) in its statistics panel. Start here: https://finance.yahoo.com/quote/AAPL. That label is a convention statement: sixty months, monthly returns, against the S&P 500.

On the day this was written it showed Apple 1.085, Coca-Cola 0.342, Exxon 0.175. My ten-year betas from Part 3 are 1.134, 0.432, 0.862. Exxon's disagrees by a factor of five.

Work the discrepancy in the order that finds it fastest:

  1. Identity. Same security? Yes.
  2. Frequency. Monthly both. Matches.
  3. Window. Mine: 2015-07 to 2025-06, 120 months. Yahoo: the sixty months ending in the most recent full month. Does not match.
  4. Return type. Mine: excess returns. Yahoo: almost certainly raw. On monthly data this moves beta by about 0.01.
  5. Regressor. Mine: French Mkt-RF. Yahoo: S&P 500 index. SPY and the index give the same beta to two decimals; French's broader market differs slightly.
  6. Formula. OLS slope both.

Step 3 is the gap. Yahoo does not print its end month, so I tried the sixty months ending in each recent full month. Sixty months ending July 2026, raw returns, against SPY, matched:

              mine, matched window    Yahoo Beta (5Y Monthly)    gap
AAPL                 1.087                    1.085             0.002
KO                   0.334                    0.342             0.008
XOM                  0.172                    0.175             0.003

Closed, provisionally: the agreement identifies the window, and the vendor has not confirmed it. The filled verification entry, the shape, not your answer:

Metric:       Exxon beta
Mine:         0.862 — monthly excess returns on French Mkt-RF, 2015-07 to 2025-06, 120 months
Source:       Yahoo Finance quote page, "Beta (5Y Monthly)", retrieved 2026-09-07: 0.175
Gap:          0.687 — not a rounding gap

              window finding, not an error, and it goes in Step 9 of the lab.

Notice what the entry does. It fails to match, says so, finds the cause by working a list, closes the gap on matched conventions, and reports both numbers with their windows. "Verified ✓" with nothing behind it is not an entry.

A gap that survives all six checks is a computation error, in one of you. Only then look at the code.

Part 8 — What your catalog now forbids

Your Week 2 catalog stated what your panel cannot be used for. Alpha is where it bites.

If your name list was built from today's constituents, every name in it survived to today. Names that were delisted, acquired at a discount, or went bankrupt are absent, and those names had, on average, negative alphas. The alphas you compute on survivors are biased upward by an amount you cannot see in your data.

The three honest responses, unchanged from Week 2:

  1. Fix the input. Rebuild the panel from a point-in-time membership list. On the professional lane this is CRSP, whose monthly file carries the delisted names and their delisting returns.
  2. Bound the damage. Estimate how many names would have been in a point-in-time list and were not, and report the alpha with that attrition rate beside it.
  3. Restrict the claim. Report the alpha as an alpha on survivors, which is a real statement as long as it says so.

What is not a response is computing the number, knowing what is wrong with it, and reporting it plainly. A Blocker open in your catalog means no headline alpha this week.
