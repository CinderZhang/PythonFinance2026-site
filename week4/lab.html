Lab 4 — Build the Optimizer, Then Break It

Register: DO. Teaching is in handout.md; conventions in rules.md.

You arrive with: your panel, stated conventions, a covariance estimate, a benchmark, and your statement of what your numbers can and cannot support. From the session you have the two-window weight swing, the out-of-sample comparison against 1/N, and the list of practitioner remedies.

Where this goes next: Week 5 turns your portfolio into an allocation policy with a risk report. The constraints you choose here are the ones you will have to justify to a committee.

Step 1 — Build the unconstrained optimizer

Using your Week 3 estimates, compute tangency weights on your panel.

Report the weights, the gross leverage (sum of absolute weights), the largest long, and the largest short. State the in-sample Sharpe ratio.

Do not fix anything yet. If the weights look absurd, that is the assignment working.

Step 2 — Move the window

Split your history into two adjacent windows. Re-estimate and re-optimize on each.

Produce the weight comparison table: each asset, weight in window A, weight in window B, and the swing in percentage points. Report gross leverage for both.

Then answer: which asset moved most, and did anything about that company plausibly change by that much between the two windows?

Step 3 — The out-of-sample test

Estimate on the earlier window. Hold those weights, unchanged, through the later window. Compare against 1/N on the same later window.

Report for both: annualized return, annualized volatility, Sharpe ratio.

State plainly which won and by how much. If your optimizer beat 1/N, that is an interesting result and you should say why you think it happened — a short window, a particular universe, or luck. Do not assume you made a mistake, and do not assume you did not.

Step 4 — Diagnose it

In a short paragraph, explain mechanically why the unconstrained optimizer behaved as it did on your panel. Refer to your own numbers, not the handout's.

At minimum address: how many parameters you estimated, how many observations you had per parameter, and where the optimizer concentrated its bets.

Step 5 — Choose one remedy and apply it

Pick one response from the handout's table — or one it does not list, if you can defend it — and apply it to your panel.

Report: the new weights, the new gross leverage, the new out-of-sample comparison against 1/N.

Then state what your remedy cost you. Every one of them gives up something — in-sample Sharpe, flexibility, the ability to express a view. Name what yours gave up.

A remedy applied without naming its cost is a remedy you do not understand.

Step 6 — Your constraints, your defence

You chose the constraints in Step 5. Now defend them.

A colleague says your constraints are arbitrary — that you capped positions at some number you picked, and a different cap would give a different portfolio, so the result is really your choice rather than the data's.

They are right that it is your choice. That is not the same as being wrong. Write the strongest version of their objection, then answer it or concede it.

Conceding earns full marks. So does answering it well. Pretending the choice was not a choice earns nothing.

Step 7 — What did you notice that I did not ask about?

The standing question.

Something in this week's optimization behaved unexpectedly. An asset that never gets weight regardless of window. A remedy that helped less than you thought. A pattern in which assets the optimizer loves. Something about how your covariance estimate changes with frequency.

Write it down with evidence.

Graded on genuineness and evidence, never on whether it was the right thing to notice.

If you want to do this differently

You may depart from the method above — a different objective function, a different remedy, a different way of testing out-of-sample. A defended departure earns full marks. State what you did instead, why, and what you gave up.

If you believe the 1/N comparison in the handout is unfair to the optimizer and can argue it, this is the place. That argument exists in the literature and it is worth making well.

Two sources, minimum — and where you have professional access, one of them is the professional source. This week that is WRDS / CRSP, for deep history — estimation error is a function of how much of it you have.

Pending access is neither an excuse nor a penalty. Document the attempt — source, route tried, date, barrier, and why that source would have been independent — and use a second free source instead. The attempt counts; the outcome never does.
