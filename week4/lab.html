Step 0 — Load what Week 3 left you

Load your excess-return series and your covariance matrix. Check three things and print the evidence: the matrix is square with one row per name, its diagonal equals your Week 3 volatilities squared, and it is symmetric. Then count your parameters. The shape, not your answer:

names 8   months per window 60
parameters: 36 covariances + 8 means = 44      months per parameter: 1.36  (a complexity count, not degrees of freedom)

Any surprise here is a Week 3 problem surfacing late. Fix it now.

Step 1 — Build the unconstrained optimizer

Split your window into two adjacent halves. On the first half, compute tangency weights on excess returns. Print the sum of the raw weights before scaling; if it is not clearly positive, stop and report that. Report the weights, the gross leverage, the largest long, the largest short, and the in-sample Sharpe ratio, labelled in-sample. The shape, not your answer:

window A 2015-07..2020-06:  largest long MSFT 100.6%   largest short XOM −52.9%   gross 2.62
in-sample Sharpe 2.234   (1/N on the same window: 0.993)

Do not fix anything yet. If the weights look absurd, that is the assignment working.

Step 2 — Move the window

Re-estimate on the second half. Produce the table: each name, weight on A, weight on B, swing in percentage points, and gross leverage for both.

Then answer in two sentences: which name moved most, and did anything about that company plausibly change by that much between the two windows?

Step 3 — Out of sample against 1/N

Hold the window-A weights through window B, reset monthly to the fixed weights, no re-estimation. Do the same for 1/N. Report annualized excess return, annual volatility, Sharpe ratio, and gross leverage for both. The shape, not your answer:

window B:  tangency-from-A  Sharpe 0.434  gross 2.62     1/N  Sharpe 1.163  gross 1.00

State plainly which won and by how much, and compare it with the prediction you wrote in prework. If your optimizer beat 1/N, say why you think it happened: a short window, a particular universe, or luck. Do not assume you made a mistake, and do not assume you did not.

Step 4 — Long-only, with your cap

Apply the remedy: no shorts, no name above the cap you declared in prework. Check first that N × cap is at least 1. Estimate on A, test on B. Report the constrained weights, how many names are held, gross leverage, the in-sample Sharpe, and the out-of-sample comparison against 1/N. Then fit the constrained portfolio on B as well, only to measure the swing; the performance test uses the A weights alone. The shape, not your answer:

cap 20%:  held 7 of 8   gross 1.00   in-sample Sharpe 1.420 (was 2.234)   out-of-sample Sharpe 0.948 vs 1/N 1.163
largest swing A→B, constrained fits on each: 20.0 pp (was 81.4 pp)

Then state what the remedy cost you, in two parts: the in-sample Sharpe you gave up, and the view you can no longer express. A remedy applied without naming its cost is a remedy you do not understand.

Step 5 — Your cap, your defence, and the portfolio Week 5 inherits

You chose the cap in prework. Now defend it.

A colleague says your cap is arbitrary: you picked a number, a different number gives a different portfolio, so the result is your choice rather than the data's. They are right that it is your choice. That is not the same as being wrong. Write the strongest version of their objection, with the number that supports it, then answer it or concede it. Conceding earns full marks. So does answering it well. Pretending the choice was not a choice earns nothing. Say whether you chose the cap before or after seeing the weights.

Then produce the portfolio Week 5 will use: long-only with your cap, estimated on your full window. Save the weights and, beside them, the constraints that produced them and the gross leverage. You name the file; Week 5 loads what you saved.

Step 6 — What did you notice that I did not ask about?

The standing question.

Something in this week's optimization behaved unexpectedly. A name that never gets weight regardless of window. A cap that helped less than you thought, or more. A pattern in which names the optimizer loves. An in-sample number that looked too good, and was.

Write it down with evidence. Graded on genuineness and evidence, never on whether it was the right thing to notice.

If you want to do this differently

You may depart from the method above. A defended departure earns full marks. State what you did instead, why, and what you gave up.

Two departures are welcome and worth a Step 6 finding: minimum variance instead of tangency, which drops the noisiest input; or the same experiment on a longer history from CRSP, which is the paper's own remedy. If you believe the 1/N comparison is unfair to the optimizer and can argue it, this is the place. That argument exists in the literature and it is worth making well.

Two sources, minimum, and where you have professional access, one of them is the professional source. This week that is CRSP monthly returns on WRDS, for history.

Pending access is neither an excuse nor a penalty. Document the attempt, source, route tried, date, barrier, and why that source would have been independent, and use a second free source instead. The attempt counts; the outcome never does.
