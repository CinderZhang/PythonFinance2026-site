Register: TEACH. The bridge from Lab 3 to what Lab 4 assumes.

You arrive with: a panel, conventions, verified metrics, a benchmark, your excess-return series, and its covariance matrix.

Part A — Load and sanity-check your estimates

Load the excess-return series and the covariance matrix you saved in Week 3. Check three things:

  1. Does the matrix have one row and one column per name, in the same order as the return columns?
  2. Are the diagonal entries your Week 3 total volatilities, squared? They should be, since the diagonal is variance. If not, you have a convention mismatch, and this is the time to find it.
  3. Is the matrix symmetric?

Any surprise here is a Week 3 problem surfacing late. Fix it now, not during the lab.

Part B — Count your parameters

Arithmetic, and it takes five minutes.

  • Names in your panel: N.
  • Distinct covariance entries: N(N+1)/2.
  • Expected returns: N.
  • Total parameters: the two added together.
  • Observations per estimation window: the months in half your history.
  • Observations per parameter: divide.

For the eight-name worked example that is 44 parameters, 60 months, 1.36 months per parameter. It is a complexity count, not a degrees-of-freedom count, since every month informs every estimate; it is still the number to be uneasy about. Write yours down and bring it.

Part C — Choose your cap before you see any weights

This matters and the order matters.

The remedy this week is fixed: no short positions, and no single name above a cap. The cap is yours, with one arithmetic floor: N × cap must be at least 1 or no fully invested portfolio exists, and at exactly 1/N the only feasible portfolio is equal weight. Write down the number now, before you optimize anything, and one sentence on why that number for your panel. Something like: with twelve names, a 15% cap forces at least seven of them into the portfolio, which is what I want.

It is very easy to choose a cap after seeing an ugly result and present it as principled. Choosing now makes your defence in Lab 4 Step 5 honest, and you will be asked whether you decided before or after.

Part D — Read the paper's argument

DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal (2009), Review of Financial Studies 22(5). Start here: https://www.lib.purdue.edu → Databases → JSTOR. DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhm075.

Read the abstract and the introduction. Then find the table that compares Sharpe ratios across strategies and datasets, and read the 1/N row against the others. Answer in one sentence each:

  1. How many months of history do they say, in their simulations, a sample-based optimizer needs before it is expected to beat 1/N on 25 assets?
  2. In their own words, why does 1/N do so well?

Bring both sentences. They are the first thing Tuesday asks.

Part E — Write one prediction

You estimate a maximum-Sharpe portfolio on five years of your panel and hold it, weights fixed, for the next five years. You compare it to an equal amount in each name. Which does better out of sample, and by roughly how much? Commit to an answer.

Write it down. Do not hedge. A specific wrong prediction is worth far more to you here than a vague safe one. You will be asked to raise your hand for one side in the session.

Bring to the session: your loaded Week 3 files, your observations-per-parameter number, your cap with its reason, your two paper sentences, and your prediction.
