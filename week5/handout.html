You arrive with: four weeks of habits about where numbers come from, and a regression from Week 3 that turns sixty monthly returns into a beta. Nothing else is assumed.

The course turns here. Portfolios are finished. From now to the end it is one question: what is a company worth, and what would have to be true for you to be wrong?

This week answers it once, on one company, all the way through. Next week you do it on your own two. Everything in this handout is worked on Asbury Automotive Group, the training case from Video 1, and every digit is reproduced by week5_worked.py, which pulls ABG from the SEC and prints the tables.

Why this matters

Valuation is where analysts are hired and where they are caught out. Not because the arithmetic is hard — a discounted cash flow is about a dozen lines, and any AI will write one in seconds — but because the answer is only as good as five inputs, and almost nobody checks where those five came from.

The question that separates a junior from a professional is not "what is your target price." It is "where did that number come from, and what would move it?"

The paper

Kaplan and Ruback (1995), "The Valuation of Cash Flow Forecasts: An Empirical Analysis", Journal of Finance 50(4), 1059–1093.

They took fifty-one leveraged transactions from 1983 to 1989 where the buyers' own cash-flow forecasts became public, discounted those forecasts, and compared the result with what was actually paid. The discounted values landed within about ten percent of the transaction values on average, with a wide spread around that average. Valuations from comparable-company multiples did about as well, and the two together did better than either.

That is the idea this week runs on, and it sets your expectations correctly: a discounted cash flow built from real forecasts lands in the neighbourhood, not on the point. A multiple is the check you run beside it. The arithmetic of the paper is thirty years old and the method has moved on; the finding has not.

Read: the abstract and the introduction. On Wiley Online Library through Purdue Libraries. Start here: https://www.lib.purdue.edu → Databases → Wiley Online Library. DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.1995.tb04050.x.

Where the method went. Damodaran (2005), "Valuation Approaches and Metrics: A Survey of the Theory and Evidence", Foundations and Trends in Finance 1(8), 693–784, maps everything that followed. One line in the references; the survey is enough.

The theory, names only. The terminal value you compute below is the Gordon–Shapiro (1956) growing perpetuity. The reason a valuation does not depend on whether cash reaches shareholders as dividends or buybacks is Miller and Modigliani (1961). Neither is required reading.

Part 1 — Why discount at all

A dollar in 2030 is worth less than a dollar today, and the cost of equity is the exchange rate.

That is the whole of it. If you require 10% a year to hold this equity, then a dollar arriving in one year is worth 1 / 1.10 = 90.9 cents to you today, and a dollar arriving in five years is worth 1 / 1.10⁵ = 62.1 cents.

So a company that will hand you a stream of cash is worth the sum of that stream, each year converted at its own exchange rate. ABG's five projected years, at 10%:

year   free cash flow to equity   × discount factor   =  present value
2026            211.4                   0.9091               192.2
2027            255.1                   0.8264               210.8
2028            299.7                   0.7513               225.2
2029            320.9                   0.6830               219.2
2030            342.3                   0.6209               212.5
                                                    sum =  1,059.9

Do the 2026 row by hand now. 211.4 ÷ 1.10 = 192.2. If you do the first one by hand, the other four stop being magic, and the rest of this week is arithmetic you already understand.

Notice the present values rise across these five years even as the discount factor falls, because the cash flow grows faster than the factor shrinks. That is specific to these numbers, not a rule. Do not use "present values should shrink" as a check on your model; whether they shrink depends on the growth rate against the discount rate, and there is no general answer.

Part 2 — Every discounted cash flow has exactly five inputs

Strip away the spreadsheet and this is all there is:

# Input ABG
1 the cash flows, for an explicit forecast window 211.4, 255.1, 299.7, 320.9, 342.3, over five years
2 the discount rate 10%
3 the growth rate after the window ends 2.5%
4 the bridge to the shareholder none needed on this route; see Part 3
5 the share count 17,951,349

Everything else in a valuation model exists to produce input 1. Everything else in a valuation argument is about inputs 2 and 3.

The rule this gives you. If you cannot say where each of the five came from, you do not have a valuation. You have a number.

Part 3 — Which cash flow, and why

There are two standard routes, and the difference is who the cash belongs to.

Cash flow to the firm Cash flow to equity
Whose cash everyone who funded the business, lenders included shareholders only, after lenders are paid
Discount at the weighted average cost of capital the cost of equity
You get enterprise value, then you subtract net debt to reach equity equity value directly
Extra step the bridge from enterprise value to equity value none

Both are correct and, done consistently, both give the same answer. This course takes the equity route, because the taught case makes the choice for us and the reason generalises:

A car dealer's financing is inseparable from its operations. ABG borrows to hold the cars on its lot — that is floor-plan financing, and it is working capital wearing a lender's clothes. Cash flow to equity, discounted at a levered cost of equity, prices the shares directly and avoids a bridge that would have to decide whether floor plan is debt or operations. Every answer to that question is arguable, so the honest move is not to need one.

Free cash flow to equity, in words: net income, plus the non-cash charges you added back, less what the business must reinvest, plus what it borrows net of what it repays. For ABG in 2026 that is 413.6 net income, plus 82.4 depreciation and 120.0 impairment, less 250.0 capital spending, less the change in working capital, plus the change in floor-plan financing, less the 150.0 of debt repaid. It comes to 211.4.

If you value a company whose financing is genuinely separate from its operations — most of them — the firm route is the more standard choice, and it is a welcome departure next week provided you do both your companies the same way.

Part 4 — The discount rate, and how little you know it

The cost of equity is built, not typed. The standard construction:

cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium

Beta is the one you compute. It is Week 3's regression, on the company instead of your portfolio: sixty monthly returns against the market's, and the slope is the beta.

For ABG, over sixty months to August 2026:

beta                0.71
standard error      0.28
R²                  0.10
ABG monthly vol    10.3%      market 4.6%      correlation 0.32

Read those four numbers together, because they are the honest part. A standard error of 0.28 on an estimate of 0.71 puts the true beta somewhere between about 0.2 and 1.3 at conventional confidence. Carry that in your head every time someone quotes a beta to two decimal places. The R² of 0.10 says the market explains a tenth of ABG's month-to-month movement; nine tenths is the company.

Two standard adjustments, both defensible, neither required:

  • Blume, which pulls the estimate toward one because betas historically drift that way: two-thirds of your estimate plus one-third of 1.0, giving 0.81 for ABG.
  • Bottom-up, which averages the betas of comparable listed companies, strips out each one's leverage, and re-levers the average at your company's own debt-to-equity. For ABG that route gives 1.03.

With a 5% risk-free rate and a 4.42% equity premium, those three betas give costs of equity of 8.15%, 8.57% and 9.54%. The base case's 10% quietly assumes a beta of 1.13, which is higher than any of them, and saying so out loud is the difference between an assumption and a mistake.

The rule this gives you. The discount rate always wants the levered beta, at the leverage you are actually forecasting. And whatever you treat as debt on one side, treat as debt on the other, or the rate contradicts the cash flow.

Part 5 — The terminal value, and the hard boundary

Your forecast stops at year five. The company does not. Everything after the window is one number, and the standard way to get it assumes the cash flow grows forever at a constant rate:

terminal value = the year after the window's cash flow ÷ (discount rate − growth rate)

For ABG, with one wrinkle worth understanding. The base case repays 150 of debt a year, but only during the explicit window — a finite repayment programme, not a perpetual one. So the terminal year's cash flow gets that 150 back before growing:

terminal cash flow   (342.3 + 150.0) × 1.025        =     504.6
terminal value       504.6 ÷ (0.10 − 0.025)         =   6,727.8
discounted 5 years   6,727.8 × 0.6209               =   4,177.5

The denominator is a hair-trigger. It is the gap between two numbers you chose, and as the growth rate approaches the discount rate the gap goes to zero and the value goes to infinity. On ABG, at a 10% discount rate:

g = 2.0%      $   276
g = 5.0%      $   417
g = 8.0%      $   979
g = 9.5%      $ 3,788
g = 9.9%      $18,772
g = 10.0%     division by zero
g = 11.0%     the arithmetic returns −$1,831 a share

That last line is the one to remember. At a growth rate above the discount rate the formula does not fail loudly; it returns a confident negative number for a profitable company. The arithmetic ran. The valuation is meaningless. This is why the model you build on Thursday refuses to run when the growth rate is not below the discount rate: a model that lets you type an impossible input will hand you an answer, and you will not notice.

What growth rate is defensible? Below the long-run growth rate of the economy the company operates in, because a company growing faster than its economy forever eventually becomes the economy. And a forecast whose growth never fades is a claim that competition never arrives — that claim needs evidence, not a spreadsheet.

Part 6 — "Most of the value is in the terminal value"

Add ABG up:

present value of the five explicit years      1,059.9
present value of the terminal value           4,177.5
equity value                                  5,237.3
÷ 17,951,349 shares                         = $291.75 a share
terminal value as a share of the total           79.8%

Nearly eighty percent of the answer is the number after the forecast stops. You will hear that called normal for a going concern, and you should be suspicious of that reassurance, because it is too loose to be useful.

Here is the part that is rarely said. Run the same business, the same drivers, the same everything, and only lengthen the explicit forecast window:

 5-year window     $291.75 a share      terminal share  79.8%
 8-year window     $290.25              terminal share  68.3%
10-year window     $288.94              terminal share  61.2%
15-year window     $285.35              terminal share  46.0%

The value barely moves. The terminal share collapses. The terminal share is partly an artifact of how long your forecast is, not a measurement of where the value comes from. It is still worth reporting, because a reader needs to know how much of your number rests on a single growth assumption, but do not treat it as a property of the company, and do not congratulate yourself for lowering it by forecasting further.

The rule this gives you. Report the terminal share every time, and report your forecast horizon beside it. Neither means anything alone.

Part 7 — What actually moves the answer

One assumption at a time, the whole model rerun each time — never a sensitivity table added up. ABG, per share:

cost of equity   8.15%  8.57%   9%   9.42%  9.54%   10%    11%
                 $398   $368   $342   $319   $313   $292   $254

The discount rate alone moves the value by about $145 a share, which is half of the answer, and none of those seven rates is unreasonable. That spread is your honest range before a single operating assumption is touched.

Then the operating drivers, each shocked alone:

gross margin  ±1 point        $256 .. $327     swing $71
capital spending  ±50         $258 .. $325     swing $67
revenue growth  ±1 point      $270 .. $314     swing $44

The market was pricing ABG near $183 in mid-September 2026. Your model says $292. That gap is the finding, and the useful response is not to defend the model but to ask what the market must be assuming: a cost of equity of 13.8%, or terminal growth of −3.3%, or a gross margin of 14.1%. Each of those is a sentence about the world, and deciding which you believe is the analysis.

Part 8 — The card

Five rows. This is the whole method, and it fits on an index card.

Step Formula ABG
1 · forecast cash flow, each year of the window 211.4 → 342.3 over five years
2 · discount CF ÷ (1 + r)ᵗ sum = 1,059.9 at r = 10%
3 · terminal final CF × (1 + g) ÷ (r − g) 504.6 ÷ 0.075 = 6,727.8
4 · add Σ present values + present value of terminal 1,059.9 + 4,177.5 = 5,237.3
5 · per share equity value ÷ shares 5,237.3m ÷ 17,951,349 = $291.75

And the four things to say every time you report one: the range, not the point; the terminal share with the horizon beside it; the dated price you are comparing against; and the single assumption that would move it most.
