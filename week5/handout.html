Why this matters

You have built a portfolio you can defend. Now imagine handing it to someone who has to hold it: a portfolio manager, a client, a committee. They do not ask for your Sharpe ratio. In the first five minutes they ask three things.

What is this actually a bet on? What does it cost to keep it the way you built it? How bad did it get, and how do you know?

A junior analyst who can answer those three questions on one page, with numbers, is trusted with the next portfolio. One who cannot is asked to go and find out. This week you find out, on the portfolio you already have, with the regression you already know how to run.

Everything this week is three small computations: a regression you already know how to run, a turnover table that is arithmetic, and a wealth path. None of them is new mathematics. The judgment is in reading the results.

The paper

This week is anchored on Fama and French (1993), "Common risk factors in the returns on stocks and bonds", Journal of Financial Economics 33(1), 3–56.

Week 3 regressed one stock's excess return on the market's excess return and got a beta, an alpha, and a split of risk into the part the market explains and the part it does not. Fama and French added two more columns to that regression: SMB, the return of small companies minus big ones, and HML, the return of high book-to-market companies, value, minus low, growth. Their finding was that those three series together explain most of the variation in the returns of diversified stock portfolios, far more than the market alone, and that after those three are accounted for the remaining unexplained return, the alpha, is usually close to zero.

Read: the abstract and the introduction. Nothing else is required; the tables are there if you want to see the R² column move when the two factors are added. The paper is on ScienceDirect through Purdue Libraries. Start here: https://www.lib.purdue.edu → Databases → ScienceDirect. DOI 10.1016/0304-405X(93)90023-5.

The framing. Andrew Ang's Asset Management: A Systematic Approach to Factor Investing (Oxford, 2014) puts it in one sentence: factors are to assets what nutrients are to food. You do not eat protein; you eat things that contain it. You do not hold "the value factor"; you hold companies whose returns move with it. A portfolio's exposures are its nutritional label. That is what you write this week.

What the paper does not say. It does not say that the factors are guaranteed to pay, or that you should build a factor portfolio. This week you read the label. You do not cook.

Textbook. The free-lane counterpart, with code, is in the DRIVER textbook: Session 5 — Portfolio Metrics for the regression and for drawdown, and Session 7 — Asset Allocation and Rebalancing for rebalancing and drift.

Part 1 — What the portfolio is a bet on

The portfolio is the Week 4 training case: the eight names, long-only, cap 20%, estimated on the full ten-year window. Six names are held: Apple 14%, Microsoft 20%, Coca-Cola 8%, JPMorgan 20%, Procter & Gamble 19%, Walmart 20%. Johnson & Johnson and Exxon are at zero. Gross leverage 1.00.

Its monthly return is the weighted sum of the eight monthly returns, weights fixed and reset each month, the same convention Week 4 used. Subtract the risk-free rate and regress on the French factors. The equation is Week 3's with two more terms:

R_p − RF  =  α  +  β_M (Mkt−RF)  +  β_S SMB  +  β_H HML  +  ε

Every number below comes from week5_worked.py, which reads the Week 3 panel file and the Week 4 weights.

Read the portfolio's row as its label.

Market beta 0.83. Over these ten years, when the market moved one percent, this portfolio moved on average about 0.83 percent in the same direction. It is a slightly defensive equity portfolio, because Coca-Cola, Procter & Gamble and Walmart pull the beta down.

Size −0.39, and the t-statistic says it is not noise. A negative loading on small-minus-big means the portfolio behaves like big companies. Of course it does: it is eight of the largest companies in the country, and a committee already knows that. What the number adds is a sentence they can use: on average in this sample, other things equal, a year in which small caps beat large caps by ten points went with this portfolio lagging the market by about four. A loading is an average association over the window, not a forecast for any particular year.

Value −0.00. No tilt either way, consistent with the growth names, Apple and Microsoft, offsetting the value names, JPMorgan and the consumer staples. That is worth saying because it is not what most people would guess from the tickers.

R² 0.82. The three factors explain 82% of the month-to-month variation in this sample. The market alone explained 75%. Look at the SPY row for scale: an index fund is 99.6% explained, because it is the market. Your portfolio is not an index fund, and the label says by how much.

The number that should make you pause

The alpha. 6.5% a year, with a t-statistic of 3.3, which by the usual rule is "significant." A junior analyst puts that on the page as evidence the portfolio adds value.

Do not. Remember what produced these weights. Week 4's optimizer chose them on this exact window to maximize the Sharpe ratio on this exact window. The alpha is measured on the same 120 months the optimizer was fitted to, and the optimizer's whole job was to make those 120 months look good. It is in-sample, in the precise sense Week 4 taught. The 1/N row, which estimated nothing, still shows 4.2%, which tells you how much of the "alpha" is the decade these particular eight companies had rather than anything anyone chose. Label it in-sample and say what produced it. That sentence is worth more to a committee than the number.

What diversification did, in one line

Week 3 split each stock's risk into the part the market explains and the rest. For these eight names the market explained on average 30% of the variance, with a range from 12% to 55%. For the portfolio built from them, the market explains 75%.

portfolio annual variance 0.0197  =  market part 0.0147  +  idiosyncratic part 0.0049
in volatility terms:   14.0%  total ;  12.1% from the market ;  7.0% idiosyncratic

The company-specific risk did not go away. It went from being most of each stock's risk to being a quarter of the portfolio's, because eight companies' surprises partly cancel. That is what diversification is, in numbers rather than as a slogan, and it is why the committee's first question is about the market exposure: after diversification, the market is most of what is left.

Part 2 — What factor investing is, in one page

You will hear "factor investing" constantly in the industry, so here is what it means and what to be careful about, without building anything.

The idea follows from the regression. If most of a diversified portfolio's return comes from a handful of factors, you can hold the factors deliberately rather than by accident. A factor fund does exactly that: it buys the stocks that load on a factor, small companies, cheap companies, companies whose price has been rising, profitable companies, low-volatility companies, and holds them systematically. Many of the largest asset managers run such funds, and a "multi-factor" fund combines several.

Factor The long side The short side, or the underweight Published where
Market equities cash Sharpe (1964), Week 3
Size small companies big companies Fama and French (1993)
Value high book-to-market low book-to-market Fama and French (1993)
Momentum past twelve-month winners past losers Carhart (1997)
Profitability, investment profitable, conservative firms unprofitable, aggressive firms Fama and French (2015)
Low volatility low-beta stocks high-beta stocks Frazzini and Pedersen (2014)

Three things a careful reader knows.

The premiums are historical averages, not promises. Every factor above has had decade-long stretches of underperformance. Value lost to growth for most of the 2010s, which is part of why your portfolio's value loading is zero without anyone deciding it should be.

Published premiums shrink. McLean and Pontiff (2016, Journal of Finance) measured what happens to a return pattern after the academic paper that documents it appears: on average it fell by more than half. What is left is smaller, noisier, and shared with everyone who read the same paper.

A factor fund is still an estimation problem. Week 4's lesson applies twice: the factor loadings are estimated with error, and the factor premiums are estimated with worse error. A multi-factor fund is a portfolio of estimates.

What you do with this in the course: read exposures, on your own portfolio, and say what they mean. What you do not do: build a factor strategy. If you want to, the Week 3 French file has everything you need and Lab 5's departure section is where it goes.

Part 3 — What keeping it costs

Your Week 4 portfolio has target weights. Prices move every month, so the actual weights drift away from the targets. Rebalancing trades them back. Every trade costs something, and you promised in Week 4 to say how often you would do it.

The cost of a trade has three parts. Commissions, which for US large-cap equities at a retail broker are now close to zero. The bid-ask spread and market impact, which are paid on every trade, are invisible on a statement, and grow with the size of the order. Tax on realised gains, in a taxable account, which is often the largest of the three and is not in any of the numbers below.

The arithmetic is short. Turnover is how much of the portfolio you trade: at each rebalance, sum the absolute changes in weight and halve it, so that selling 5% of Microsoft to buy 5% of Coca-Cola is 5% turnover, not 10%. Add it up per year. Drag is annual turnover times the round-trip cost of a trade, in basis points, bp, hundredths of a percent.

Four policies on the same weights over the same ten years, starting at the targets and letting prices do what they did:

policy resets one-way turnover per year drag at 10 bp round trip drag at 50 bp annual return vol Sharpe largest drift from target biggest position at the end
monthly 119 20.8% 2.1 bp 10.4 bp 19.5% 14.0% 1.255 3.4 pp JPM 21.4%
quarterly 39 12.5% 1.2 bp 6.2 bp 19.5% 14.0% 1.259 5.0 pp MSFT 24.0%
annual 9 5.8% 0.6 bp 2.9 bp 19.4% 14.1% 1.245 7.0 pp JPM 24.1%
never 0 0 0 0 19.9% 15.1% 1.194 23.3 pp MSFT 40.5%

Returns and Sharpe ratios are before costs and, as always for this window, in-sample.

Read it in two directions.

Down the cost column, the answer is: it is cheap. For eight of the most liquid stocks in the world, monthly rebalancing at a plausible 10 basis points round trip costs about two basis points a year. Even at 50 basis points, a number you would use for a less liquid name, it is ten. The return columns barely move between monthly, quarterly and annual. If you expected rebalancing frequency to be a big decision for this portfolio, the table says it is not.

Down the drift column is the reason to rebalance at all. Never touching the portfolio cost nothing and, over ten years, let Microsoft grow from 20% to 40.5% of it. Twice the cap. The cap you chose in Week 4 prework and defended in Lab 4 was silently gone within a few years, and the portfolio at the end of the decade was not the portfolio you defended. It was a Microsoft position with seven passengers.

So the rebalancing decision for a portfolio like this is not about return or cost. It is about staying the portfolio you said you would hold. Quarterly keeps every name within 5 points of target for about one basis point a year. That is the sentence to write.

One precision. The cap binds the target weights. Between resets the holdings drift, by up to the drift column: under quarterly resets Microsoft touched 24% before being trimmed back. If a committee wants the cap to hold on the holdings every day, a calendar is the wrong tool and the threshold policy below is the right one.

Where the cost is not small: portfolios of small or thinly traded names, where round trips run to hundreds of basis points; strategies with high turnover by design, where 400% turnover at 10 basis points is 40 basis points a year before the strategy has done anything; and taxable accounts, where every rebalance realises gains. Your panel decides which world you are in, and the cost number you cite in prework is your evidence.

A policy that is not on the table. Threshold rebalancing trades only when a weight drifts past a band, say 5 points from target. It trades only when needed and keeps every holding within the band, which is what a cap on holdings, rather than on targets, requires. The band is a parameter you choose and defend, exactly like the cap was. It is welcome as a departure in Lab 5.

Part 4 — How bad did it get

The third question. Two numbers answer it, each with its dates, and both are already in your return series.

worst month maximum drawdown peak to trough months to recover
portfolio 2020-02, −8.5% −21.3% 2021-12 to 2022-09 7
1/N 2020-02, −9.6% −18.0% 2020-01 to 2020-03 5
SPY 2020-03, −12.5% −23.9% 2021-12 to 2022-09 15

Worst month is what it says. Maximum drawdown is the largest fall from a previous high to a later low, measured on the wealth path, and it is the number a client actually experienced: someone who bought this portfolio at the end of 2021 was down 21% nine months later and waited another seven to get back to even. That is a different fact from "annual volatility 14%," and committees ask for it because they have lived through it.

The recipe is four lines, on the monthly total returns, not the excess returns, because a client's wealth compounds the whole return:

1. wealth = (1 + r).cumprod()      one dollar compounded through the months
2. peak   = wealth.cummax()        the highest value reached so far
3. dd     = wealth / peak − 1      how far below that high, every month
4. max drawdown = dd.min()         its month is the trough; the peak is the last high before it;
                                   recovery = months from the trough until wealth first regains the peak,
                                   or "not recovered by window end"

With digits, for the portfolio. Wealth reached 3.887 at the end of 2021 and fell to 3.061 by September 2022: 3.061 / 3.887 − 1 = −21.3%. It regained 3.887 seven months later. The COVID episode was the second deepest, 2.479 to 2.120 over two months, −14.5%, which is why "worst month" and "maximum drawdown" name different episodes here: the worst single month was February 2020, but the deepest hole was dug slowly in 2022. Over the ten years the portfolio spent 55 of 120 months below a previous high. That last number surprises most people, and it belongs on the page.

Three conventions, stated so the number means the same thing to everyone. It is computed on monthly observations, so the low inside a month is not seen; the true March 2020 bottom was deeper than any month-end. It is computed on the monthly-reset series, the Week 4 convention, whatever rebalancing policy the sheet recommends; if you want the drawdown of the quarterly path, compute it on that path and say so. And it is a record of what happened, never a limit on what can happen: the next drawdown can be deeper than the worst one so far, and a sheet that reads "maximum drawdown −21%" as a floor has misread it.

What else exists, names only. Value at Risk and expected shortfall, which turn the tail of the distribution into one number; stress tests, which ask what a named scenario would do. On ten years of monthly data they rest on a handful of observations. The textbook covers them for anyone who wants them. Not on the lab.

Part 6 — What a professional dataset changes here

The factor series you regressed on are free, from Ken French's library, and they are the reference implementation. The professional version of this week is about history and breadth. A factor exposure estimated on 120 months has wide error bands; the same regression on the CRSP monthly file adds decades of observations, provided the names existed then and their exposures did not change, which is the question to ask before trusting the narrower band. And a size loading of −0.39 means something different if the universe it is measured in is eight survivors than if it is the whole market with delistings kept. Week 2's problem, once more, propagates into every coefficient.
