A dollar in 2030

A dollar in 2030 is worth less than a dollar today.

The cost of equity is the exchange rate.

If you require 10% a year:

a dollar in 1 year   →   1 / 1.10    =  90.9 cents today
a dollar in 5 years  →   1 / 1.10⁵   =  62.1 cents today

A company that hands you a stream of cash is worth the sum of that stream, each year converted at its own rate.

That is the whole idea. Everything else is working out the stream.

year     FCFE      × factor      =   present value

211.4 ÷ 1.10 = 192.2. Do it now. Once the first one is yours, the other four stop being magic.

The present values rise here, because the cash flow grows faster than the factor shrinks. That is these numbers, not a rule. Never check a model against "PVs should shrink."

Every DCF has exactly five inputs

# Input ABG
1 the cash flows, over an explicit window 211.4 → 342.3, five years
2 the discount rate 10%
3 growth after the window 2.5%
4 the bridge to the shareholder none on this route
5 the share count 17,951,349

Everything else in the model exists to produce input 1.

Everything else in the argument is about inputs 2 and 3.

If you cannot say where each of the five came from, you do not have a valuation. You have a number.

Which cash flow, and why

to the firm to equity
whose cash everyone, lenders included shareholders, after lenders
discount at weighted average cost of capital cost of equity
you get enterprise value, then bridge equity value, directly

Both correct. Done consistently, same answer.

We take the equity route, because ABG makes the choice for us:

A dealer borrows to hold the cars on its lot. Floor-plan financing is working capital wearing a lender's clothes. The equity route prices the shares directly and avoids a bridge that would have to rule on what floor plan is — and every answer to that is arguable.

FCFE = net income + non-cash charges − reinvestment + net borrowing. ABG 2026: 413.6 + 82.4 + 120.0 − 250.0 − ΔWC + Δfloor plan − 150.0 = 211.4

The discount rate is built, not typed

cost of equity = risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium

Beta is Week 3's regression, on the company: 60 monthly returns against the market, and the slope.

ABG        beta 0.71     standard error 0.28     R² 0.10
           ABG vol 10.3%/mo    market 4.6%    correlation 0.32

Read all four together. A standard error of 0.28 on 0.71 puts the true beta somewhere near 0.2 to 1.3.

Carry that in your head every time someone quotes a beta to two decimals.

R² of 0.10: the market explains a tenth of ABG's movement. Nine tenths is the company.

Three betas, three rates

beta cost of equity $/share
observed 0.71 8.15% 398
Blume-adjusted (⅔ est + ⅓ of 1.0) 0.81 8.57% 368
bottom-up, re-levered at D/E 1.03 1.03 9.54% 313
the base case 1.13 implied 10% 292

The base case's 10% quietly assumes a beta of 1.13 — higher than all three.

Saying that out loud is the difference between an assumption and a mistake.

The rate always wants the levered beta, at the leverage you are forecasting. Whatever is debt in the cash flow is debt in the rate.

The terminal value

Your forecast stops at year five. The company does not.

terminal value = next year's cash flow ÷ (r − g)
terminal cash flow   (342.3 + 150.0) × 1.025   =     504.6
                      ↑ the repayment is finite — restore it
terminal value       504.6 ÷ (0.10 − 0.025)    =   6,727.8
discounted 5 years   × 0.6209                  =   4,177.5

The denominator is a hair-trigger

ABG, discount rate held at 10%:

g =  2.0%      $    276
g =  5.0%      $    417
g =  8.0%      $    979
g =  9.5%      $  3,788
g =  9.9%      $ 18,772
g = 10.0%      division by zero
g = 11.0%      −$1,831 a share

Look at the last line. Above the discount rate the formula does not fail loudly — it returns a confident negative number for a profitable company.

The arithmetic ran. The valuation is meaningless.

That is why Thursday's model refuses when g ≥ r. A model that answers an impossible question answers it confidently, and you will not notice.

"Most of the value is in the terminal value"

PV of five explicit years      1,059.9
PV of the terminal value       4,177.5
equity value                   5,237.3   ÷ 17,951,349  =  $291.75
terminal share                    79.8%

You will hear 80% called normal for a going concern. Be suspicious — it is too loose to be useful.

Same business, same drivers. Only the window changes:

 5-year window    $291.75     terminal share 79.8%
10-year window    $288.94     terminal share 61.2%
15-year window    $285.35     terminal share 46.0%

The value barely moves. The share collapses.

The terminal share is partly an artifact of your horizon, not a measurement of where value comes from. Report it — with the horizon beside it. Neither means anything alone.

What actually moves it

cost of equity  8.15%  8.57%   9%   9.42%  9.54%   10%    11%
                $398   $368   $342   $319   $313   $292   $254

$145 a share from the discount rate alone, and none of those seven is unreasonable. That is your honest range before a single operating assumption moves.

gross margin ±1 pt     $256 .. $327     swing $71
capital spending ±50   $258 .. $325     swing $67
revenue growth ±1 pt   $270 .. $314     swing $44

Market: about $183, mid-September 2026. Model: $292.

The gap is the finding. What must the market assume? A 13.8% cost of equity, or g = −3.3%, or a 14.1% gross margin. Each is a sentence about the world.
