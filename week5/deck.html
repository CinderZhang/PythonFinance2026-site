The question

You hand the portfolio to someone who has to hold it. In the first five minutes they ask:

What is this a bet on?

What does it cost to keep it the way you built it?

How bad did it get, and how do you know?

Three small computations: a regression you already know, a turnover table, a wealth path. No new mathematics. The judgment is in the reading.

The paper

Fama & French (1993), Common risk factors in the returns on stocks and bonds, Journal of Financial Economics.

Week 3's regression, with two more columns: SMB (small minus big) and HML (value minus growth).

Three series explain most of a diversified portfolio's return. After them, the alpha is usually near zero.

The framing — Ang (2014): factors are to assets what nutrients are to food. You read the label. You do not cook.

What diversification did

This portfolio: 75%.

Company-specific risk did not vanish. It went from most of each stock to a quarter of the portfolio. That is diversification, in digits.

The number that should make you pause

α = 6.5% a year, t = 3.3.

Who chose these weights? Week 4's optimizer, on this exact window, to make this exact window look good.

In-sample, in the precise sense Week 4 taught. 1/N, which chose nothing, shows 4.2%.

Label it. Say what produced it. That sentence is worth more than the number.

Factor investing, in one slide

Hold the factors on purpose instead of by accident: size, value, momentum, profitability, low volatility. Many of the largest managers run such funds.

Three things a careful reader knows:

  1. The premiums are historical averages, with decade-long droughts. Value lost to growth for most of the 2010s.
  2. Published premiums shrink — McLean & Pontiff: by more than half, on average, after the paper appears.
  3. A factor fund is still an estimation problem. Week 4, twice over.

This course: read the label on your own portfolio. Not on the lab: building a factor strategy.

What keeping it costs

Same weights, ten years, four policies. Drag = one-way turnover × round-trip cost.

policy turnover / yr drag at 10 bp Sharpe largest drift end
monthly 20.8% 2.1 bp 1.255 3.4 pp JPM 21%
quarterly 12.5% 1.2 bp 1.259 5.0 pp MSFT 24%
annual 5.8% 0.6 bp 1.245 7.0 pp JPM 24%
never 0 0 1.194 23.3 pp MSFT 40%

Down the cost column: for eight mega-caps, it is cheap. The return barely moves.

Down the drift column: "never" carried MSFT to twice your cap. The portfolio you defended was gone within a few years. The cap binds targets; holdings drift between resets (quarterly: MSFT touched 24%).

Rebalancing is about staying the portfolio you said you would hold. Not priced here: tax.
