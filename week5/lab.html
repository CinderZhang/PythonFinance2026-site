Lab 5 — The Risk Report

Register: DO. Teaching is in handout.md; conventions in rules.md.

You arrive with: a portfolio, its constraints, its gross leverage, and your diagnosis of why the unconstrained version could not be trusted. From the session you have the VaR backtest showing 1.77x the advertised breach rate, the clustering, the unbounded tail, and the seven items a committee needs.

Where this goes next: Week 6 values a company and Week 7 assembles everything into one deliverable. The limitations section you write here is reused there almost verbatim — so write it as though someone will read it, because someone will.

Step 1 — Compute VaR three ways

For your portfolio, compute 1-day 99% VaR by all three methods: historical, parametric, and one simulation-based approach.

Report all three side by side. They will disagree. State which you will carry forward and why.

Then state, in one sentence each, the assumption each method makes and where you expect it to fail.

Step 2 — Backtest your own VaR

Run the rolling backtest on your portfolio. Report:

  • number of observations tested
  • expected breaches at your confidence level
  • actual breaches
  • the ratio
  • breaches by year

Check your shift. If your breach rate comes in at or below the advertised level, that is the first thing to suspect — a VaR computed with same-day data will look excellent and be worthless.

Step 3 — Characterise the tail

Two things the breach count hides.

  1. Clustering. Report breaches by year. Is your worst year consistent with independent

breaches at your stated rate, or not? Say which, and show the numbers.

  1. Magnitude. Table your five worst breaches: actual loss, VaR limit, excess in percentage

points. Then compute CVaR — the average loss conditional on breaching — and compare it to your VaR.

State the sentence a committee would need to hear about your worst case.

Step 4 — Price your rebalancing policy

State your policy — calendar, threshold, or never — chosen in prework before you priced it.

Then estimate:

  • the annual turnover it implies
  • a round-trip cost assumption, and where you got it
  • the resulting annual drag in basis points

Compare against at least one alternative policy. Report the drag for both and say what the more expensive one buys you.

If you conclude the cheaper policy is better, say so. A well-argued "do less" is a real finding, and it is right more often than students expect.

Step 5 — Name what your risk report cannot see

Using the handout's blind-spot table and your own data_quality_issues.md, write the limitations section.

For each limitation: what it is, why this report cannot see it, and what someone relying on this report should therefore not conclude.

Include model risk explicitly, and use your own Step 2 backtest ratio as the evidence. You measured it. Report it.

Step 6 — Decide the action in advance

State what you would do if the risk limit is breached — before it is breached.

Be specific and realistic. "Reduce exposure" is not an action. Which positions, by how much, on what trigger, and who decides?

Then answer: what would have to be true for this plan to be the wrong response? Every pre-committed action has a scenario in which following it makes things worse. Name yours.

Step 7 — Your policy, your defence

You chose the rebalancing policy and the risk limits.

A committee member says your limit is set where it is because it is comfortable, not because it is right — and that a tighter limit would have caught the drawdown you just showed them.

Write the strongest version of their case, with the number that supports it. Then answer it or concede it. Conceding earns full marks.

Step 8 — What did you notice that I did not ask about?

The standing question.

Something this week behaved unexpectedly. A method that disagreed with the others more than you thought. A year whose breach count made no sense. A cost that dominated the others. A relationship between leverage and breach frequency.

Write it down with evidence.

Graded on genuineness and evidence, never on whether it was the right thing to notice.

If you want to do this differently

You may depart from the method above — a different risk measure, a different backtest design, a different horizon. A defended departure earns full marks. State what you did instead, why, and what you gave up.

If you think VaR is the wrong tool for your portfolio entirely and can argue it, that argument is welcome and worth making well.

Two sources, minimum — and where you have professional access, one of them is the professional source. This week that is WRDS / CRSP, and TRACE if your portfolio carries credit, for long history for tail estimates, and transaction-level bond data a free feed does not hold.

Pending access is neither an excuse nor a penalty. Document the attempt — source, route tried, date, barrier, and why that source would have been independent — and use a second free source instead. The attempt counts; the outcome never does.
