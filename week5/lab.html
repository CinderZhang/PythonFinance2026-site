Part 1 — Tuesday (not collected)

Step 0 — One row by hand

On paper or in one cell, compute the present value of ABG's 2026 free cash flow to equity at a 10% cost of equity.

Known answer: 211.4 ÷ 1.10 = 192.2.

Then the other four years, and add them. Known answer: 1,059.9.

Then the terminal value: (342.3 + 150.0) × 1.025 ÷ (0.10 − 0.025), discounted five years. Known answer: 6,727.8 before discounting, 4,177.5 after.

Add the two. Divide by 17,951,349 shares. Known answer: $291.75.

That is the entire method, done by hand, in ten minutes. Everything Thursday adds is the machinery that produces the five cash flows honestly and proves they are not invented.

Step 2 — The company layer, and the projection (35 min)

Two halves, kept apart deliberately. The company half holds ABG's filed lines and its drivers. The method half holds the projection, the checks and the valuation, and knows nothing about which company it is running. Next week you replace only the first half, twice.

Send:

Build me a five-year pro-forma for Asbury Automotive Group, fiscal 2026 to 2030, in $ millions, in two clearly separated parts: a COMPANY dictionary holding the opening balance sheet and the drivers, and METHOD functions that take that dictionary and know nothing about ABG. Opening balance sheet at 2025-12-31: revenue 17,999.0, inventory 2,135.8, PP&E 3,070.4, cash 40.4, floor plan 2,027.0, debt 3,572.0, revolver 0, equity 3,891.7, other assets 6,371.6, other liabilities 2,127.5. Drivers: organic revenue growth 1.8%; gross margin 17.05%; SG&A as a share of gross profit 66.5%, 65.5%, then 64.5% for years three to five; depreciation 2.68% of opening PP&E; a non-cash asset impairment of 120 a year; capital spending 250 a year; tax 25.5%; inventory 52.2 days of cost of sales; floor plan 94.9% of inventory; other working capital 0.8% of the change in revenue; floor-plan interest 4.67% and term-debt interest 5.44% on opening balances; a revolver at 6% capped at 850; minimum cash 25; term-debt repayment 150 a year; buyback 150 a year. Income statement: revenue, gross profit, SG&A, depreciation, the impairment, EBIT, interest on opening balances, tax, net income. Balance sheet: inventory from days, floor plan from the ratio, PP&E rolling with capital spending less depreciation, debt falling by the repayment, equity rolling with net income less the buyback, and other assets rolling with the working-capital change less the impairment. Free cash flow to equity is net income plus depreciation plus the impairment less capital spending less the change in inventory less other working capital plus the change in floor plan less the repayment. Cash is computed last from opening cash plus that flow less the buyback, drawing the revolver only if cash would fall below the minimum. Print the five years.

Known answers, 2026: revenue 18,323.0 · gross profit 3,124.1 · SG&A 2,077.5 · EBIT 844.2 · interest 289.0 · net income 413.6 · free cash flow to equity 211.4 · cash 101.8 · revolver 0. 2030: net income 527.5 · free cash flow to equity 342.3 · cash 719.8. The path is 211.4, 255.1, 299.7, 320.9, 342.3.

If a number differs, walk the income statement down until you find the first line that does. That line is the fix. Do not start from the share price.

Step 3 — The check block (20 min)

A projection you cannot check is a guess with decimals.

Under the projection, print a check block for every year: total assets minus total liabilities minus equity; cash on the balance sheet minus what the cash-flow statement says it should be; and cash minus the minimum. Make it a function that returns True only when all three pass in every year, within a tenth of a million.

Known answer: the balance line reads 0.0 in every year, and all five years pass.

Then break it on purpose, and put it back. Unlink cash from the cash-flow statement for one year and rerun: the residual appears immediately. Relink it. A check that cannot fail is not a check, and you have now seen yours fail.

Step 4 — The valuation, and the cell that refuses (20 min)

Discount the five years of free cash flow to equity at a 10% cost of equity. Compute the terminal value as the final year's flow plus the final year's 150 repayment, restored because the repayment is finite, times 1.025, over 0.10 minus 0.025, discounted five years. Add them, divide by 17,951,349 shares, and print the present value of the five years, the discounted terminal value, equity value, value per share, and the terminal value's share of the total. Raise an error before any arithmetic if terminal growth is at or above the discount rate, or if the check block does not pass.

Known answer: 1,059.9 + 4,177.5 = 5,237.3, $291.75 a share, 79.8% terminal. Within a dollar, or an input differs from the list in Step 2.

Two probes, both quick, both required. Set terminal growth to 10% and rerun: the cell must raise, not return infinity. Break one line of the check block and rerun the valuation: it must refuse. A model that answers an impossible question will answer it confidently, and you will not notice.

Then put the dated market price beside your number: ABG's close on the day you run this, with its date and source written down. The video's marker was about $183 in mid-September 2026; yours is yours.

If you want to do this differently

You may depart from the method above. A defended departure earns full marks: state what you did instead, why, and what you gave up.

The welcome one this week is the firm route — free cash flow to the firm, discounted at a weighted average cost of capital, bridged to equity with dated net debt. It is the more standard choice for a company whose financing is separate from its operations, and the handout's Part 3 says why ABG is not that company. If you take it, say what you decided floor-plan financing is, and why.

Two sources, minimum, and where you have professional access one of them is the professional source. This week that is Compustat beside SEC EDGAR as filed. Pending access is neither an excuse nor a penalty: document the attempt and use the filing. The attempt counts; the outcome never does.
