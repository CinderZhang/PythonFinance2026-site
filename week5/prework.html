Week 5 — Prework

Register: TEACH. The bridge from Lab 4 to what Lab 5 assumes.

You arrive with: a long-only portfolio with your cap, saved with its constraints and gross leverage, and one sentence on how often you intend to rebalance it.

Honest time budget: about 55 minutes.

Part A — Build the series and check it

Load two things you already have: the weights you saved in Lab 4 Step 5, and the Week 3 panel with the French columns joined to it. Multiply each month's returns by the weights and sum across names. That is your portfolio's monthly return with the weights fixed and reset each month, the convention Week 4 used.

Check three things and print the evidence:

  1. The weights sum to one and none is negative or above your cap. If not, you have loaded the wrong file.
  2. The series has one row per month of your window and no gaps.
  3. Its annual mean and volatility match what Lab 4 reported for the full-window portfolio, once you compare like with like: Week 4 reported excess returns, so subtract the risk-free column before comparing. The volatility barely changes; the mean differs by the average risk-free rate.

Any surprise here is a Week 4 problem surfacing late. Fix it now, not in the lab.

Part B — Choose your rebalancing frequency

Decide now, before you price anything: monthly, quarterly, or annual. Write one sentence on why that frequency for your panel.

The order matters for the same reason it did with the cap. In Lab 5 Step 3 you will be asked whether you chose before or after seeing the cost, and the honest answer is required. Choosing after is allowed and must be disclosed. Choosing now gives you a cleaner defence.

Part C — Find a real transaction-cost number

Find an estimate of the round-trip cost of trading the kind of security in your portfolio, in basis points. Not a number you assumed. One you can cite.

Places to look: your broker's published commission and spread disclosures; a fund's annual report, which discloses trading costs; academic estimates of spreads and market impact for US equities. Start here: https://www.lib.purdue.edu → Databases → JSTOR, and search for effective spread estimates for US stocks.

Write the number and where it came from. If you cannot find one, write where you looked and what you will assume instead. An assumption labelled as an assumption is fine. An assumption presented as a fact is not.

Part D — Read the paper's argument

Fama and French (1993), Journal of Financial Economics 33(1). Start here: https://www.lib.purdue.edu → Databases → ScienceDirect. DOI 10.1016/0304-405X(93)90023-5.

Read the abstract and the introduction. Nothing else is required. Answer in one sentence each:

  1. What are the two factors they add to the market, and what does each one measure?
  2. In their own words, what happens to the intercepts, the alphas, once all three factors are in the regression?

Bring both sentences. They are the first thing Tuesday asks.

Part E — Write one prediction

Before you run anything on your own portfolio, write down two numbers: its market beta, and the share of its monthly variance you think the market explains. Then one word for each of size and value: positive, negative, or none.

Commit. A specific wrong prediction is worth far more to you here than a vague safe one, because the gap between your guess and the regression is exactly what a committee will ask you to explain.

Bring to the session: your portfolio series with its three checks, your rebalancing frequency with its reason, your cited cost number, your two paper sentences, and your prediction.
